Unsupervised Learning: Autoencoders, VAEs, GANs and Diffusion

Labels are usually the most expensive part of a dataset. Unsupervised learning uses the data alone. An autoencoder learns to squeeze its input through a small code and rebuild it, which gives compression, features and denoising. Generating new data is harder: you can't sample from the distribution of real images, but you can sample noise from $\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)$ and learn to transform it into data. A VAE decodes noise in one pass, a GAN trains a generator against a discriminator, and a diffusion model removes noise one small step at a time. Judging whether the results are any good is still an open problem.

0Test yourself first

Answer out loud before you read on. Each answer points to the part that explains it.

1. You trained a plain autoencoder. Can you make a brand-new image by typing random numbers into its code $\mathbf h$ and running the decoder?
Not reliably. Nothing says which values of $\mathbf h$ decode to something sensible: the codes of the training images can sit anywhere, with empty gaps in between (slide 11). A VAE fixes this by pushing the codes towards $\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)$, so you know what to sample. → Part 6
2. In the GAN objective, which network maximises, which minimises, and what value does each want $D(G(\mathbf z))$ to have?
The discriminator maximises and wants $D(G(\mathbf z))\to0$ (and $D(\mathbf x)\to1$ on real data). The generator minimises and wants $D(G(\mathbf z))\to1$: fool the discriminator. → Part 7
3. A diffusion model: does the network learn to add noise or to remove it? Where does generation start?
Adding noise is a fixed recipe: nothing to learn. The network learns the step back, a denoising step. Generation starts from pure noise $\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)$ and takes many small denoising steps. (2025 MC 14, one you missed.) → Part 8

One sentence to remember

An autoencoder learns $g(f(\mathbf x))\approx\mathbf x$ through a bottleneck or from noisy inputs. Generative models learn to turn samples of $\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)$ into samples that look like the data: a VAE by decoding a code that is kept close to $\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)$, a GAN by fooling a discriminator, a diffusion model by removing noise one small step at a time.

2Why unsupervised? slide 2 DL book 14, 20

The questions of slide 2
  • What is unsupervised learning? Learning without a ground-truth label: only the inputs $\mathbf x_i$.
  • Why is that good? Labels are often the most expensive part to obtain: someone has to annotate every image. Unlabelled data are cheap and plentiful.
  • What is it good for?
    • Compression: store a large dataset in fewer numbers.
    • Pre-training for feature learning: learn features on lots of unlabelled data, then train a classifier on a few labels.
    • Density estimation: how likely is this sample? A very unlikely one is an anomaly.
    • Generating new data samples.

Topics of this lecture: autoencoders; generative models (VAE, GANs, diffusion); evaluation.

The trick that makes it trainable

An autoencoder uses the input as its own target (Jordan's blog). The unlabelled problem becomes an ordinary supervised one, so everything you know (a loss, backprop, SGD) still works.

3Autoencoders slides 3–4 DL book 14.1 · Jordan's blog · Bishop & Bishop 19.1

Formula (slide 3)

A feed-forward network that reproduces its input at the output layer, $g(f(\mathbf x))=\mathbf x$:

$$\text{encoder: }\mathbf h=f(\mathbf x)=\sigma(\mathbf b+\mathbf W_h\mathbf x),\qquad\text{decoder: }\mathbf r=g(\mathbf h)=\sigma(\mathbf c+\mathbf W_r\mathbf h)$$

Train it by minimising the reconstruction error over the training samples (for continuous $\mathbf x$):

$$\mathcal L=\sum_i\tfrac12\big\|g(f(\mathbf x_i))-\mathbf x_i\big\|^2$$
  • $\sigma$ is any activation function; the slides write column vectors ($\mathbf W\mathbf x$). With the batch in rows, as in our code, it is $\mathbf X\mathbf W$.
  • For pixels in $[0,1]$ you can also use binary cross-entropy per pixel instead of the squared error (Week 3b).
Q: Explain what you see (slide 3)
  • Input and output have the same size; the layer in the middle is smaller.
  • Everything the decoder knows about $\mathbf x$ has to pass through $\mathbf h$. So training forces $\mathbf h$ to keep what matters about the input.
  • No labels anywhere: the loss compares the output with the input.
Tiny numbers: a linear 2-1-2 autoencoder

Encoder $h=0.6\,x_1+0.8\,x_2$, decoder $\mathbf r=h\,(0.6,\;0.8)$, no activations.

$\mathbf x=(2,\;1)$: $h=1.2+0.8=2.0$, so $\mathbf r=(1.2,\;1.6)$. Error $(-0.8,\;0.6)$, loss $\tfrac12(0.64+0.36)=0.5$.

$\mathbf x=(1.5,\;2)$: $h=0.9+1.6=2.5$, so $\mathbf r=(1.5,\;2.0)$: perfect, loss 0.

A one-number code keeps one direction, here $(0.6,0.8)$. Inputs along it come back exactly; the part across it is lost. That is what PCA does with one component (exercise E1).

How big should the code be? slide 4

What could stop an overcomplete autoencoder from copying? (slide 4)

Regularisation (Week 5b): give the network a reason not to learn the identity. The next part does it with noise; Jordan's blog lists the main options below.

From the blog: Jeremy Jordan, "Introduction to autoencoders" (linked on slide 3)
  • The balance: the model must be sensitive enough to the input to rebuild it, yet insensitive enough not to memorise the training data. So the loss is "reconstruction + regulariser", with a weight on the regulariser.
  • Linear autoencoder ≈ PCA. Without activation functions an undercomplete autoencoder finds the same subspace as PCA; nonlinear layers make it a nonlinear generalisation of PCA that can follow curved data (figure below; DL book 14.1 says the same).
  • A small code isn't enough on its own. Very powerful encoders and decoders could memorise even through a single number, by mapping each training sample to its own index. Limit their capacity too.
  • Applications: anomaly detection (unusual inputs reconstruct badly), denoising images or audio, inpainting (filling in missing parts), and information retrieval (search by code).

4Denoising autoencoders slides 5–6 DL book 14.2.2, 14.5 · Jordan's blog

Formula (slide 5)

Idea: regularisation by making the reconstruction robust to noise. Generate a noisy $\tilde{\mathbf x}$ from a distribution $p(\tilde{\mathbf x}\mid\mathbf x)$, for example

$$\tilde{\mathbf x}=\mathbf x+\sigma\boldsymbol\epsilon,\quad\boldsymbol\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)\qquad\text{or: set random entries of }\mathbf x\text{ to }0.$$

Q: How do you make it robust to noise? A: put the noisy sample in and reconstruct the noise-free one, $g(f(\tilde{\mathbf x}))=\mathbf x$:

$$\mathcal L=\sum_i\tfrac12\big\|g(f(\tilde{\mathbf x}_i))-\mathbf x_i\big\|^2$$
Slide 6 (DL book fig. 14.4): the arrows

The crosses are training samples. They lie on a curve: the manifold of real data. The noise process $C(\tilde{\mathbf x}\mid\mathbf x)$ kicks a sample off the curve (the grey circle shows how far it can go). The trained denoising autoencoder maps it back, so the vector $g(f(\tilde{\mathbf x}))-\tilde{\mathbf x}$ points from the noisy point towards the manifold. Together these arrows form a vector field that points towards the data. The widget computes that field for the best possible denoiser.

The "best possible denoiser" returns the average of all clean points that could have produced $\tilde{\mathbf x}$, which is what a perfectly trained denoising autoencoder learns. Errors are mean squared distances to the true clean point. Try: at $\sigma=0.2$ the arrows point straight back to the curve and the error roughly halves: the part of the noise across the curve is removed, but the part along it can't be undone, since the denoiser can't know where along the curve the point came from. Switch to the vector field: every arrow points to the manifold, like slide 6. Push $\sigma$ to 1: the noise wipes out the information and the denoiser can only send everything towards the middle of the data.

Remember this arrow: it comes back in diffusion

For Gaussian noise, the best denoiser's arrow divided by $\sigma^2$ is the score of the noisy data (Part 8):

$$\frac{g(f(\tilde{\mathbf x}))-\tilde{\mathbf x}}{\sigma^2}\;\approx\;\nabla_{\tilde{\mathbf x}}\log p_\sigma(\tilde{\mathbf x}).$$

That is why slide 19 answers "what type of network does diffusion use?" with "a denoising autoencoder".

Tiny numbers: what a denoiser can and can't undo

All clean data lie on the line $x_2=0$. A clean point $(1,\;0)$ gets noise $(0.2,\;-0.3)$, so $\tilde{\mathbf x}=(1.2,\;-0.3)$.

The best denoiser returns $(1.2,\;0)$: it removes the $-0.3$ across the line, but the $0.2$ along the line looks like data, so it stays. Squared error before: $0.04+0.09=0.13$; after: $0.04$.

From the blog: denoising vs contractive (Jordan)
  • A denoising autoencoder makes the reconstruction robust to small but finite corruptions. A contractive one makes the encoder insensitive to tiny changes of the input, by penalising the Jacobian $\partial\mathbf h/\partial\mathbf x$.
  • The arrows are only trustworthy near the training data. Far away there were no training examples, so the reconstruction error can be large and point anywhere. That problem returns in Part 8.
  • Choose the noise level so that the signal survives but copying no longer pays off.

5Generating data from noise slides 7–8, 10, 12, 17

The questions of slide 8
  • What are the blue and red dots? Cat and dog images: samples from a distribution over pixels (drawn in 1-D).
  • You can't sample from this distribution directly to make new cat or dog images: nobody knows its formula, and it lives in a space with one dimension per pixel.
  • Key idea: transform samples from a distribution you can sample from: $\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)$.
  • What are the green dots? "Noise images": samples of $\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)$.

This is the main idea of all generative approaches in this lecture: VAE, GANs, diffusion.

Tiny numbers: a transformation makes easy samples look like data

If $z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$, then $x=3+0.5\,z$ follows $\mathcal N(3,\;0.5^2)$. Draws $z=(-1.2,\;0.3,\;2.0)$ give $x=(2.4,\;3.15,\;4.0)$.

For images the transformation is a neural network with millions of parameters instead of $3+0.5z$, but the recipe is the same: draw $\mathbf z$, compute $\mathbf x=G(\mathbf z)$.

6Variational autoencoders slides 10–11 DL book 20.9, 20.10.3 · Jordan's VAE post · Bishop & Bishop 19.2

Q: How could you sample from an autoencoder, with no input? (slide 11)

You can't. You don't know which values to put in $\mathbf h$: its range is undefined. Idea: make the hidden layer a distribution that is easy to sample from, $\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)$. Slide 10 puts it as: a VAE uses $\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)$ to sample hidden representations.

From the blog: Jordan's intuition

Describe each latent attribute with a range of values instead of a single number. Picture a face model with a few latent attributes such as skin tone, glasses, or how much the person smiles. For an ambiguous expression (think of the Mona Lisa) a single "smile" value is hard to pick; a mean with a spread says "somewhere around here". Because the decoder has to work for any sample from that range, nearby codes must decode to similar faces. That makes the latent space smooth and continuous, which is exactly what you need to generate.

Formula: the VAE

The encoder outputs a mean and a spread per latent dimension, a Gaussian with diagonal covariance:

$$q(\mathbf z\mid\mathbf x)=\mathcal N\big(\boldsymbol\mu(\mathbf x),\operatorname{diag}\boldsymbol\sigma^2(\mathbf x)\big),\qquad\mathbf z=\boldsymbol\mu+\boldsymbol\sigma\odot\boldsymbol\epsilon,\quad\boldsymbol\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)\;\;\text{(slide 11)}$$

The loss has two terms (Jordan's post):

$$\mathcal L=\underbrace{\big\|\mathbf x-g(\mathbf z)\big\|^2}_{\text{reconstruction}}+\underbrace{D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(\mathbf z\mid\mathbf x)\,\big\|\,\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)\big)}_{\text{keep the codes near the prior}}$$

For Gaussians the KL term has a closed form, summed over the latent dimensions $j$:

$$D_{\mathrm{KL}}=\tfrac12\sum_j\big(\mu_j^2+\sigma_j^2-1-\ln\sigma_j^2\big)\qquad(=0\text{ only for }\mu_j=0,\;\sigma_j=1)$$

In practice the encoder predicts $\log\sigma^2$, which can be any real number, and uses $\sigma=\exp(\tfrac12\log\sigma^2)$, which is always positive (Jordan).

Tiny numbers: one latent dimension

The encoder gives $\mu=0.6$, $\sigma=0.5$; we draw $\epsilon=1.2$. Sample: $z=0.6+0.5\cdot1.2=1.2$.

KL: $\tfrac12\big(0.36+0.25-1-\ln0.25\big)=\tfrac12(0.36+0.25-1+1.386)=0.498$.

Compare: $\mu=0,\sigma=1$ gives $0$; $\mu=3,\sigma=0.1$ gives $\tfrac12(9+0.01-1+4.61)=6.31$. Codes far from 0, or very narrow ones, are expensive.

The grey dots are 24 fixed draws $\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,1)$; each line maps one of them to $z=\mu+\sigma\epsilon$. Try: move $\mu$ and $\sigma$: the same $\epsilon$s slide and stretch smoothly, so $z$ has a derivative with respect to both ($\partial z/\partial\mu=1$, $\partial z/\partial\sigma=\epsilon$). That is the reparameterisation trick. The KL term is 0 only at $\mu=0$, $\sigma=1$, where $q$ covers the prior exactly. A tiny $\sigma$ (an almost fixed code, as in a plain autoencoder) or a large $|\mu|$ costs a lot of KL.

What each loss term does Jordan's VAE post

The price of a sampleable code (24/25 #46)

The KL term pulls every input's code towards the same $\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)$, so codes overlap more and reconstructions are usually blurrier than a plain autoencoder's. "The simpler latent density of a VAE improves reconstruction accuracy compared to a standard autoencoder" is the statement that is not correct. What the simple latent density buys is generation: 24/25 #38 ("VAEs enable sampling by forcing the latent distribution towards a simple distribution") is true.

From the blog: generating and exploring (Jordan)
  • Generate: skip the encoder, draw $\mathbf z\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)$, decode. On MNIST, decoding a grid of 2-D codes shows the digits morphing smoothly into each other.
  • Interpolate: walk in a straight line from the code of one input to the code of another; the decoded outputs change gradually. (Google's MusicVAE does this with melodies.)
  • Diagnose: if the encoder's distributions are extremely narrow, the KL term is too weak.
  • β-VAE: multiply the KL term by $\beta>1$. The latent dimensions become more independent ("disentangled": one dimension ≈ one attribute), at some cost in reconstruction.
Where the loss comes from (beyond the slides: Jordan, DL book 20.10.3)

Think of each $\mathbf x$ as generated from a hidden $\mathbf z$: first $\mathbf z\sim p(\mathbf z)=\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)$, then $\mathbf x\sim p(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf z)$, the decoder. To encode we would need $p(\mathbf z\mid\mathbf x)=p(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf z)p(\mathbf z)/p(\mathbf x)$, but $p(\mathbf x)=\int p(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf z)p(\mathbf z)\,d\mathbf z$ is intractable. Variational inference: approximate it with a simpler $q(\mathbf z\mid\mathbf x)$, the encoder, and maximise the evidence lower bound (ELBO):

$$\log p(\mathbf x)\;\ge\;\mathbb E_{q(\mathbf z\mid\mathbf x)}\big[\log p(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf z)\big]-D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(q(\mathbf z\mid\mathbf x)\,\|\,p(\mathbf z)\big)$$

With a Gaussian decoder the first term is $-\|\mathbf x-g(\mathbf z)\|^2$ up to a scale and a constant. So minimising "reconstruction + KL" maximises a lower bound on the likelihood of the data. Jordan calls the encoder the recognition model and the decoder the generative model.

Code: VAE forward and loss with basic tensor operations
# x: (N, D)   the encoder gives mu and logvar, both (N, K)
hid = torch.relu(x @ W1 + b1)
mu, logvar = hid @ W_mu + b_mu, hid @ W_lv + b_lv
eps = torch.randn_like(mu)                              # the randomness comes from outside
z = mu + torch.exp(0.5 * logvar) * eps                  # reparameterisation: (N, K)
r = torch.sigmoid(torch.relu(z @ W2 + b2) @ W3 + b3)    # decoder: (N, D)
rec = ((r - x) ** 2).sum(dim=1)                         # (N,)
kl = 0.5 * (mu ** 2 + logvar.exp() - 1 - logvar).sum(dim=1)   # (N,)
loss = (rec + kl).mean()
# generating later: z = torch.randn(n, K), then only the decoder line

7GANs: generative adversarial networks slides 12–15 DL book 20.10.4 · Cohen & Giryes · Bishop & Bishop 17

Intuition (slides 12–13)

Use $\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)$ to sample noise as input, and let a network learn the transformation from noise to the training distribution. Input white noise $\mathbf z\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)$, output an image. There is no encoder, and the generator never looks at a training image directly.

Formula (slide 15)
$$\arg\min_{\theta_G}\max_{\theta_D}\;\underbrace{\mathbb E_{\mathbf x\sim p_{\text{data}}}\log D_{\theta_D}(\mathbf x)}_{D\text{ for real data }\mathbf x}+\underbrace{\mathbb E_{\mathbf z\sim p(\mathbf z)}\log\big(1-D_{\theta_D}(G_{\theta_G}(\mathbf z))\big)}_{D\text{ for fake data}}$$
  • $D_{\theta_D}(\cdot)\in(0,1)$: the probability that the input is real (a sigmoid output).
  • Where is the discriminator? In both terms: on real data in the first, on the generator's fakes in the second.
  • It is binary cross-entropy (Week 3b) with label 1 for real and 0 for fake. Maximising it is the same as $D$ minimising its classification loss.

Tiny numbers: one real and one fake image

$D(\mathbf x)=0.9$ and $D(G(\mathbf z))=0.2$: $V=\ln0.9+\ln0.8=-0.105-0.223=-0.33$. Close to the maximum 0: $D$ is winning.

The generator improves until $D(G(\mathbf z))=0.6$: $V=\ln0.9+\ln0.4=-0.105-0.916=-1.02$. $V$ went down: $G$ is winning.

At the end of training the fakes look exactly like real data, so the best $D$ can do is say $\tfrac12$ to everything: $V=\ln\tfrac12+\ln\tfrac12=-\ln4=-1.39$.

The best discriminator (beyond the slides: the original GAN paper, Goodfellow et al. 2014)

For a fixed generator with distribution $p_g$, the best discriminator is

$$D^*(\mathbf x)=\frac{p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf x)}{p_{\text{data}}(\mathbf x)+p_g(\mathbf x)}.$$

Where real data are 3 times as likely as fakes ($0.3$ vs $0.1$), $D^*=0.75$. With $D^*$ plugged in, the generator minimises $-\ln4+2\,\mathrm{JSD}(p_{\text{data}}\,\|\,p_g)$, where JSD (the Jensen–Shannon divergence) is a symmetric relative of the KL divergence. It is smallest exactly when $p_g=p_{\text{data}}$, with $D^*=\tfrac12$ everywhere.

Code: training in turns
for x in loader:                                       # a batch of real images
    z = torch.randn(N, K)
    # 1. discriminator: real → 1, fake → 0 (binary cross-entropy = −V)
    d_loss = bce(D(x), ones) + bce(D(G(z).detach()), zeros)
    opt_D.zero_grad(); d_loss.backward(); opt_D.step()
    # 2. generator: make D say "real" on its fakes
    g_loss = bce(D(G(z)), ones)                         # = −log D(G(z)): the non-saturating loss
    opt_G.zero_grad(); g_loss.backward(); opt_G.step()

.detach() keeps the discriminator's step from changing the generator. In step 2 the gradient flows through $D$ into $G$, but only $G$'s optimiser takes a step.

What goes wrong (the GAN chapter linked on slide 13: Cohen & Giryes)
  • Mode collapse: the generator finds a few outputs that fool $D$ and keeps producing only those (say, only one kind of digit).
  • Vanishing gradients: when $D$ wins easily, the minimax loss gives $G$ almost no gradient (figure and widget above).
  • Instability: two networks chase each other; there is no single loss that steadily goes down, so it's hard to tell when to stop.
  • Fixes change the architecture (for example convolutional generators) or the loss (for example the Wasserstein GAN). Uses in the chapter: data augmentation and generating face images.
  • And from Song's blog: a GAN gives no likelihood $p(\mathbf x)$. It describes the distribution only through its sampling process.

8Diffusion & score matching slides 17–19 Song's blog · Bishop & Bishop 20, 14.3

Intuition (slide 17)

The same key idea once more: use $\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)$ to sample noise as input and transform it into an in-distribution image. But instead of one big transformation, diffusion takes many small steps, each one a little denoising.

Forward: turn data into noise (slide 19)

In $t=0,\dots,T$ steps, take an image $\mathbf x_0$ to noise $\mathbf x_T$ with mean $\mu=0$ and $\sigma=1$. The slides do it in two pieces:

$$\text{shrink to mean }0:\;\;\mathbf x_{t+1}=\mathbf x_t(1-\beta),\;\;0\le\beta\le1,\qquad\text{add noise to reach }\sigma=1:\;\;\mathbf x_{t+1}=\mathbf x_t+\beta\boldsymbol\epsilon,\;\;\boldsymbol\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)$$
  • Q: $\beta_t$ over time? $\beta_{t-1}\le\beta_t\le\beta_{t+1}$: small steps first, larger steps later.
  • Q: Is this deterministic? No: every step draws fresh noise.

In papers (DDPM, Ho et al. 2020) both pieces are one step, with square roots so that the variance ends at exactly 1, and many steps collapse into one formula:

$$\mathbf x_t=\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,\mathbf x_{t-1}+\sqrt{\beta_t}\,\boldsymbol\epsilon\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad\mathbf x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,\mathbf x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\boldsymbol\epsilon,\quad\bar\alpha_t=\prod_{s\le t}(1-\beta_s)$$
Tiny numbers: one pixel, value 2, beta 0.1 every step

Mean only ($\boldsymbol\epsilon=0$): $x_1=\sqrt{0.9}\cdot2=1.90$, $\;x_{10}=0.9^{5}\cdot2=1.18$.

With noise: $\bar\alpha_{10}=0.9^{10}=0.349$, so $x_{10}=0.59\cdot2+0.81\,\epsilon$. And $\bar\alpha_{50}=0.9^{50}=0.0052$, so $x_{50}=0.072\cdot2+0.997\,\epsilon\approx\epsilon$: pure noise. The starting value 2 is forgotten.

Reverse: learn the step back (slides 18–19)
  • Repeatedly turn all training data into noise, and learn to predict the step back, $p_\theta(\mathbf x_{t-1}\mid\mathbf x_t)$.
  • Q: What type of network? A denoising autoencoder (Part 4): it gets a noisy $\mathbf x_t$ (and the step $t$) and outputs how to move towards the data.
  • Generate: sample $\mathbf x_T\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)$ and step back $T$ times to $\mathbf x_0$.
  • The training target in practice (DDPM, beyond the slides): pick a training image $\mathbf x_0$, a step $t$ and noise $\boldsymbol\epsilon$; build $\mathbf x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\mathbf x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\boldsymbol\epsilon$; train the network to predict the noise:
$$\mathcal L=\big\|\boldsymbol\epsilon-\boldsymbol\epsilon_\theta(\mathbf x_t,t)\big\|^2.$$

Knowing the noise that was added is knowing how to step back.

The score: what the network really learns slide 19 Song's blog

Formula: score, score matching, Langevin dynamics (Song's blog)
  • The score of a distribution is $\nabla_{\mathbf x}\log p(\mathbf x)$: at every point, an arrow towards higher density. The slides write $\nabla_{\mathbf x}p(\mathbf x)$. That is the same direction, because $\nabla\log p=\nabla p/p$ and $p>0$; only the length differs. Either way: "learn how to take a step back".
  • Why the log? A density must integrate to 1, which needs a normalising constant $Z$ that is impossible to compute for images. If $p(\mathbf x)=e^{-f(\mathbf x)}/Z$, then $\nabla_{\mathbf x}\log p(\mathbf x)=-\nabla_{\mathbf x}f(\mathbf x)$: $Z$ drops out, so the network never needs it.
  • Score matching: train $\mathbf s_\theta(\mathbf x)\approx\nabla_{\mathbf x}\log p(\mathbf x)$ by minimising $\mathbb E_{p(\mathbf x)}\big\|\nabla_{\mathbf x}\log p(\mathbf x)-\mathbf s_\theta(\mathbf x)\big\|^2$ (the Fisher divergence). Tricks such as denoising score matching do this without knowing the true score: add noise of known size, and the target becomes $-(\tilde{\mathbf x}-\mathbf x)/\sigma^2=-\boldsymbol\epsilon/\sigma$. Predicting the noise again.
  • Langevin dynamics samples using only the score: walk along the arrows, plus a little noise, with a small step size $\eta$ (Song writes $\epsilon$):
$$\mathbf x_{i+1}=\mathbf x_i+\eta\,\nabla_{\mathbf x}\log p(\mathbf x_i)+\sqrt{2\eta}\;\mathbf z_i,\qquad\mathbf z_i\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)$$

With a small step and many iterations the samples follow $p(\mathbf x)$. Without the noise term every sample would just climb to the nearest peak.

Tiny numbers: one Langevin step

Target $p=\mathcal N(0,1)$, so the score is $-x$. Start at $x=3$, step $\eta=0.1$, noise draw $z=0.5$:

$x\leftarrow3+0.1\cdot(-3)+\sqrt{0.2}\cdot0.5=3-0.3+0.22=2.92$. The score pulls towards 0; the noise keeps the samples spread out like $\mathcal N(0,1)$.

From the blog: why many noise levels (Song)
  • The pitfall: score matching weights its errors by $p(\mathbf x)$, so the learned arrows are only accurate where there is data. In high dimensions, a random starting point almost surely lies in a low-density region, where the arrows are wrong (the figure above, top).
  • The fix: perturb the data with noise of several sizes $\sigma_1<\sigma_2<\dots<\sigma_L$. Large noise fills the empty space, so the arrows can be learned everywhere; small noise keeps the details. One network $\mathbf s_\theta(\mathbf x,\sigma_i)$ learns all levels (a noise-conditional score network).
  • Annealed Langevin dynamics: sample with the largest noise level first, then smaller and smaller. A diffusion model's time step $t$ plays exactly this role: each $t$ is a noise level.
  • SDE view (know it exists): with infinitely many noise levels the forward process is a stochastic differential equation, $d\mathbf x=\mathbf f(\mathbf x,t)\,dt+g(t)\,d\mathbf w$, and the reverse is $d\mathbf x=\big[\mathbf f(\mathbf x,t)-g(t)^2\nabla_{\mathbf x}\log p_t(\mathbf x)\big]dt+g(t)\,d\bar{\mathbf w}$, run backwards in time. It needs only the score at each time. Dropping the noise and halving the score term gives the probability-flow ODE: deterministic paths with the same distributions at every time, which also gives exact likelihoods.
  • Song shows that diffusion models (DDPM) and score-based models are two views of the same thing: DDPM's training loss is a weighted sum of score-matching losses.

Exam check (2025 MC 14 and 24/25 #42)

Training: corrupt data with noise (fixed) and learn the step back. Generation: start from $\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)$ and step back towards the real image using $\nabla_{\mathbf x}\log p(\mathbf x)$. "Encode into a bottleneck, sample $\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)$, decode in one shot" describes a VAE, not diffusion. The 24/25 statement "diffusion models synthesise data from random noise by iteratively applying structured noise until the samples follow the data distribution" was marked true in the review. Read it as: start from noise and transform it step by step, where each step moves along the learned score and, in the stochastic sampler, adds a little fresh noise.

Idea (slide 21)Why it isn't enough

How to evaluate generative models is an active research topic.

Tiny numbers: good likelihood, terrible samples

Mix a good model with pure noise: $p_{\text{mix}}=0.01\,p_{\text{good}}+0.99\,p_{\text{noise}}\ge0.01\,p_{\text{good}}$. So $\log p_{\text{mix}}\ge\log p_{\text{good}}-\ln100=\log p_{\text{good}}-4.6$ nats per image.

A CIFAR-10 image has $32\cdot32\cdot3=3072$ numbers; a good model scores around 3 bits per number, a log-likelihood near $-6400$ nats per image. Losing 4.6 of them is 0.07%: invisible in the score, while 99 of every 100 samples are noise. (A classic argument by Theis et al., 2016.)

What papers report (beyond the slides)
  • FID (Fréchet Inception Distance): run real and generated images through a pretrained image classifier, fit a Gaussian to the features of each set, and measure the distance between the two Gaussians. Lower is better.
  • Inception score: high when each sample is classified confidently and the classes are diverse. Higher is better.
  • Song's blog reports, on CIFAR-10, an FID of 2.20 for his score-based model against 2.92 for the best GAN of the time.
  • To catch copying, compare samples with their nearest neighbours in the training set.

10Recap

What to know for the exam (slide 22)
  • Autoencoders: $g(f(\mathbf x))\approx\mathbf x$ with loss $\sum_i\frac12\|g(f(\mathbf x_i))-\mathbf x_i\|^2$. Undercomplete codes compress; overcomplete ones need regularisation, for example denoising: input $\tilde{\mathbf x}$, target $\mathbf x$.
  • Sampling from an unknown distribution by transforming a known one, $\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)$.
  • VAE: the encoder outputs $\boldsymbol\mu,\boldsymbol\sigma$; $\mathbf z=\boldsymbol\mu+\boldsymbol\sigma\odot\boldsymbol\epsilon$; loss = reconstruction + KL to $\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)$. Generate by decoding $\mathbf z\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)$.
  • GAN: $\min_G\max_D\;\mathbb E\log D(\mathbf x)+\mathbb E\log(1-D(G(\mathbf z)))$. $D$: real → 1, fake → 0; $G$: make $D(G(\mathbf z))\to1$.
  • Diffusion / score matching: fixed noising in $T$ steps with growing $\beta_t$; learn the step back (a denoising autoencoder) along $\nabla_{\mathbf x}\log p(\mathbf x)$; generate from $\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)$ in many steps.
  • Evaluation: likelihood, human judgement and downstream tasks each fall short; it's an open problem.

11Handwriting blueprint

Write each step on paper first, then reveal it.

1 · An autoencoder forward pass and its loss

  1. Linear 2-1-2 autoencoder: $h=0.6x_1+0.8x_2$, $\mathbf r=h\,(0.6,0.8)$; input $\mathbf x=(2,1)$.
  2. Encode: $h=1.2+0.8=2.0$.
  3. Decode: $\mathbf r=(1.2,\;1.6)$.
  4. Loss: $\tfrac12\big((1.2-2)^2+(1.6-1)^2\big)=\tfrac12(0.64+0.36)=0.5$.

2 · A VAE sample and its KL term

  1. The encoder gives $\mu=0.6$ and $\log\sigma^2=\ln0.25$, so $\sigma=\sqrt{0.25}=0.5$.
  2. Draw $\epsilon=1.2$ from $\mathcal N(0,1)$; reparameterise: $z=\mu+\sigma\epsilon=0.6+0.6=1.2$.
  3. KL: $\tfrac12(\mu^2+\sigma^2-1-\ln\sigma^2)=\tfrac12(0.36+0.25-1+1.386)=0.498$.
  4. Loss = reconstruction error of $g(z)$ + 0.498. The gradient reaches $\mu$ and $\sigma$ through $\partial z/\partial\mu=1$ and $\partial z/\partial\sigma=\epsilon=1.2$.

3 · The GAN objective with numbers

  1. $V=\log D(\mathbf x)+\log(1-D(G(\mathbf z)))$; $D$ maximises, $G$ minimises.
  2. $D(\mathbf x)=0.9$, $D(G(\mathbf z))=0.2$: $V=\ln0.9+\ln0.8=-0.33$.
  3. Best discriminator: $D^*=p_{\text{data}}/(p_{\text{data}}+p_g)$; with $0.3$ and $0.1$: $0.75$.
  4. Equilibrium $p_g=p_{\text{data}}$: $D^*=\tfrac12$ everywhere and $V=-\ln4=-1.39$.

4 · Diffusion forward by hand

  1. One step: $x_t=\sqrt{1-\beta}\,x_{t-1}+\sqrt\beta\,\epsilon$; with $x_0=2$, $\beta=0.1$, $\epsilon=0$: $x_1=0.949\cdot2=1.90$.
  2. Many steps at once: $\bar\alpha_t=0.9^t$, $x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon$.
  3. $t=10$, $\epsilon=0.5$: $\sqrt{0.349}=0.590$, $\sqrt{0.651}=0.807$, so $x_{10}=1.181+0.404=1.58$.
  4. Training pair for the network: input $(x_{10}=1.58,\;t=10)$, target the noise $\epsilon=0.5$; loss $(\epsilon_\theta-0.5)^2$.

5 · One Langevin step

  1. Rule: $x\leftarrow x+\eta\,\nabla_x\log p(x)+\sqrt{2\eta}\,z$.
  2. For $p=\mathcal N(0,1)$: $\log p=-x^2/2+\text{const}$, so the score is $-x$.
  3. $x=3$, $\eta=0.1$, $z=0.5$: $3-0.3+0.447\cdot0.5=2.92$.
  4. Repeat: the score pulls towards high density, the noise keeps the spread; the samples end up distributed as $p$.

12Exam traps

Watch out
  • An autoencoder needs no labels: the target is the input itself.
  • Overcomplete without regularisation can learn the identity, which is useless. Denoising (or sparsity) prevents it (slide 4).
  • An undercomplete autoencoder compresses well only for samples like the training samples. Very different inputs reconstruct badly, which is what anomaly detection uses.
  • A linear autoencoder with squared loss ≈ PCA. Nonlinearity is what lets it follow curved data.
  • Denoising: the input is noisy and the target is clean, not the other way round.
  • Plain autoencoder codes have an unknown range, so you can't sample from them; VAEs make sampling possible (24/25 #38: true).
  • A VAE does not reconstruct better than a plain autoencoder (24/25 #46): the KL term costs reconstruction quality.
  • Reparameterisation: $\mathbf z=\boldsymbol\mu+\boldsymbol\sigma\odot\boldsymbol\epsilon$ with $\boldsymbol\sigma$ the standard deviation, not the variance. Predict $\log\sigma^2$ and take $\exp(\tfrac12\cdot)$.
  • The KL term alone maps every input to $\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)$: the codes then say nothing about the input.
  • GAN roles: $\min$ over $G$, $\max$ over $D$. $D(\mathbf x)\to1$ for real data; $G$ wants $D(G(\mathbf z))\to1$. At equilibrium $D=\tfrac12$ and $V=-\ln4$.
  • A GAN has no encoder and no likelihood. Its generator never sees training images; it learns only through $D$'s gradient.
  • Diffusion: the noising (forward) process is fixed and the reverse is learned; $\beta_t$ grows over time; the forward process is not deterministic.
  • Diffusion generation starts from $\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)$, not from an encoded image (2025 MC 14).
  • $\nabla_{\mathbf x}p(\mathbf x)$ (slides) vs $\nabla_{\mathbf x}\log p(\mathbf x)$ (the score): same direction, different length.
  • Diffusion is slow to sample: $T$ network passes, against one for a VAE or GAN.
  • A higher likelihood isn't better-looking samples, and human judges are fooled by a model that copies the training set.

13Self-check

Answer first, then open. past exam marks questions from the exam reviews.

1. past exam True or false: VAEs enable sample generation by forcing the latent vector distribution to follow a simple distribution (e.g. a normal) from which we can easily sample. (24/25 #38)
True. The KL term pushes $q(\mathbf z\mid\mathbf x)$ towards $\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)$, so after training you sample $\mathbf z\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)$ and decode.
2. past exam Which statement about the VAE is NOT correct? (24/25 #46)
"The simpler density of the latent variables in VAEs improves the reconstruction accuracy of the input image compared to standard autoencoders." The KL term pulls all codes towards the same prior, so they overlap and reconstructions get worse (blurrier), not better. The simple density is there to make sampling possible.
3. past exam Which option best describes the objective function of a GAN? (24/25 #49)
A minimax optimisation: the generator tries to minimise the discriminator's ability to tell real from fake, while the discriminator tries to maximise it: $\min_G\max_D\;\mathbb E\log D(\mathbf x)+\mathbb E\log(1-D(G(\mathbf z)))$.
4. past exam Which option best describes the training procedure of diffusion models? (2025 MC 14, missed)
"Start from a sample of $\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)$ and learn how to take a step backwards towards the real image using $\nabla_{\mathbf x}p(\mathbf x)$." The option "pass an input to the encoder into a bottleneck, sample from $\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\mathbf I)$ and decode" describes a VAE.
5. past exam True or false: diffusion models synthesise data from random noise by iteratively applying structured noise until the samples follow the distribution of the input dataset. (24/25 #42)
The review says true: generation starts from random noise and transforms it step by step. The wording is shaky, because what each step mainly does is remove noise along the learned score (the stochastic sampler also adds a little fresh noise per step). Adding noise on its own is the forward, training-time process.
6. Why doesn't a denoising autoencoder learn the identity, even when it is overcomplete?
Its input is $\tilde{\mathbf x}$ but its target is $\mathbf x$. The identity would output $\tilde{\mathbf x}$, noise included, and pay the full noise as loss. To do better it has to learn what clean data look like and move noisy points back towards the manifold.
7. An undercomplete autoencoder reconstructs your training images well. Will it reconstruct an X-ray just as well if it was trained on cats? Why is that useful?
No: it compresses well only for samples like its training data (slide 4). The large reconstruction error flags the X-ray as unusual, which is how autoencoders do anomaly detection.
8. What exactly does the reparameterisation trick change, and what stays the same?
The distribution of $\mathbf z$ stays $\mathcal N(\boldsymbol\mu,\operatorname{diag}\boldsymbol\sigma^2)$. What changes is where the randomness enters: $\boldsymbol\epsilon$ is an input, and $\mathbf z=\boldsymbol\mu+\boldsymbol\sigma\odot\boldsymbol\epsilon$ is a deterministic function of $\boldsymbol\mu$ and $\boldsymbol\sigma$, so backprop can reach the encoder.
9. Early in GAN training the discriminator easily rejects every fake. Why does the generator then barely learn with the minimax loss, and what is the usual fix?
With $D=\sigma(a)$ and $a\ll0$, $\log(1-D)$ is flat in $a$: its derivative is $-D\approx0$. The fix is to let the generator minimise $-\log D(G(\mathbf z))$ (non-saturating), whose derivative is about $-1$ there. Same goal, a strong signal.
10. Why do diffusion and score-based models use many noise levels instead of one?
Little noise keeps the data sharp, but leaves empty regions where the score can't be learned; lots of noise fills the space but blurs the data. Using all levels, from large to small, gives accurate arrows everywhere and sharp samples at the end (Song's annealing).
11. Why can't you evaluate a generative model just by asking people whether its samples look real?
A model that simply copies the training set would get a perfect score without generating anything new (slide 21). You also need to check novelty and diversity.

One pixel with $x_0=2$; noise schedule $\beta_t=0.1$ for every step; forward step $x_t=\sqrt{1-\beta_t}\,x_{t-1}+\sqrt{\beta_t}\,\epsilon$. (a) Ignoring the noise, what is the mean after 1 and after 10 steps? (b) With the closed form, compute $x_{10}$ for $\epsilon=0.5$, and describe $x_{50}$. (c) What exactly does the network get as input and as target in one DDPM training step? (d) With the slides' simpler shrink step $x_{t+1}=x_t(1-\beta)$, what is left after 10 steps? And should $\beta_t$ really be constant?

Hint

$\bar\alpha_t=\prod_{s\le t}(1-\beta_s)=0.9^t$ and $x_t=\sqrt{\bar\alpha_t}\,x_0+\sqrt{1-\bar\alpha_t}\,\epsilon$. For (d), look at slide 19's question about $\beta_t$ over time.

Show worked solution

(a) $\sqrt{0.9}=0.949$: $x_1=1.90$; after 10 steps $0.9^{5}\cdot2=1.18$.

(b) $\bar\alpha_{10}=0.349$: $x_{10}=0.590\cdot2+0.807\cdot0.5=1.181+0.404=1.58$. $\bar\alpha_{50}=0.0052$: $x_{50}=0.072\cdot2+0.997\,\epsilon$, essentially $\mathcal N(0,1)$ noise whatever $x_0$ was.

(c) Input: the noisy value and its step, $(x_{10}=1.58,\;t=10)$. Target: the noise that was added, $\epsilon=0.5$. Loss $(\epsilon_\theta(1.58,10)-0.5)^2$. Each training step picks a fresh image, step and noise.

(d) $2\cdot0.9^{10}=0.70$: the mean shrinks towards 0. And no: the slides want $\beta_{t-1}\le\beta_t\le\beta_{t+1}$, small steps first (fine detail is destroyed gently) and larger ones later, when the image is already mostly noise.
