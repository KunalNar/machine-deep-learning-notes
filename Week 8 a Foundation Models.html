Foundation Models: Pre-training and Adapting

A foundation model is one big model, trained once on broad data, that is then adapted to many different tasks. Three ideas make it work. One architecture for everything: the Transformer, which takes any signal once it is cut into tokens (text, image patches with the ViT, video). Pre-training without labels: the data supervises itself (predict the next word, rebuild hidden image patches, match images to their captions with CLIP). Cheap adaptation: freeze the huge model and train only a few new parameters (a new head, adapters, prompt tokens, or a low-rank update with LoRA).

0Test yourself first

Answer out loud before you read on. Each answer points to the part that explains it.

1. A hospital has a ViT pre-trained on millions of images and only 500 labelled X-rays. Train a new ViT from scratch, fine-tune all 86 million weights, or freeze the ViT and train a few new parameters? Why?
Usually freeze it and train a few parameters (a new head, adapters, or LoRA): parameter-efficient fine-tuning. From scratch is hopeless: a ViT has few built-in assumptions about images and needs a lot of data. Full fine-tuning can work, but 86 million weights on 500 images overfit easily, may forget what pre-training taught, and every task needs its own full copy of the model. → Part 8
2. When a GPT model is pre-trained on text, or a masked autoencoder on images, who provides the labels?
The data itself. The target for each position is the next word of the same text; for the MAE it is the hidden patches of the same image. No human labels are needed: self-supervised learning (slide 13). → Part 6
3. After fine-tuning with LoRA, is the model slower than the original at test time?
No. LoRA learns $\Delta\mathbf W=\mathbf B\mathbf A$, which has the same shape as $\mathbf W_0$, so after training you store $\mathbf W=\mathbf W_0+\mathbf B\mathbf A$ and the network is exactly as big as before. An adapter, in contrast, adds extra layers to every forward pass. → Part 9

2What is a foundation model? slides 3–7 Bommasani et al. 2021

Intuition

Until now every model in this course was trained for one task: a classifier for one dataset. A foundation model flips that. You train one very large model, once, on as much varied data as you can get. It learns general things (what words mean, what objects look like). Every new task then starts from that model and only adapts it a little.

The term comes from a 2021 Stanford report (slide 3). The definition: any model trained on broad data that can be adapted (fine-tuned) to a wide range of downstream tasks. The model is a "foundation" because many applications are built on top of it, and because everything built on it inherits its strengths and its flaws.

What the slides stress (slide 3)
  • Technically not new. Deep networks and self-supervised learning have existed for decades; transfer learning from ImageNet was standard in the CNN era. What is new is the scale and scope (e.g. ChatGPT).
  • Risks. They can amplify harms, and their characteristics are in general poorly understood.
  • Many names, one idea: pre-trained model, self-supervised model, (large) language model, general-purpose / multi-purpose / task-agnostic model, foundation model.
Emergence and homogenization (slide 4)
  • Emergence: behaviour that is implicitly induced rather than explicitly constructed. The best-known example: GPT-3 can do a new task from a few examples written in its prompt, without any training on that task (in-context learning). Nobody built that in; it appeared with scale.
  • Homogenization: consolidation of ML methods across a wide range of applications. Today almost every state-of-the-art NLP model is adapted from one of a few foundation models, and the same Transformer is used for text, images, speech and more.
Social impact (slide 6)

The report places the model in an ecosystem: data creation → data curation → training → adaptation → deployment. People sit at both ends: they create the data, and they receive the benefits and harms. The slide lists the issues: fairness (biases in the data reach every adapted model), economics (only a few organisations can afford to train these), environment (the energy cost of training), misuse (e.g. generated disinformation), legality (copyright, liability) and ethics. Deploying an adapted model is a separate decision from building it.

Slide 16 asks: where are the learnable parameters? In the token embeddings and position embeddings, in the projections $\mathbf W_q,\mathbf W_k,\mathbf W_v$ (and the output projection $\mathbf W_o$ that combines the heads), in the MLPs, in the layer norms (a scale and a shift per feature), and in the final linear layer. The softmax of $\mathbf Q\mathbf K^\top$ itself has none.

Tiny numbers: one ViT-Base block

$D=768$, MLP hidden size $4D=3072$.

  • Attention: four $768\times768$ matrices with biases: $4(768^2+768)=2{,}362{,}368$.
  • MLP: $768\cdot3072+3072+3072\cdot768+768=4{,}722{,}432$.
  • Two layer norms: $2\cdot(768+768)=3{,}072$.
  • One block: $7{,}087{,}872$. Twelve blocks: $85{,}054{,}464$, most of the model.
In code
d, h_mlp, n_blocks = 768, 3072, 12   # ViT-Base
attn = 4 * (d * d + d)               # W_q, W_k, W_v, W_o (+ biases)
mlp  = d * h_mlp + h_mlp + h_mlp * d + d
norm = 2 * (2 * d)                   # two layer norms: scale + shift
block = attn + mlp + norm
print(attn, mlp, norm, block)
print(n_blocks * block)

4Transformers on images: ViT slide 11 Dosovitskiy et al. 2021

Q: How to apply the transformer on images? (slide 11)

A Transformer needs a sequence of vectors. Taking every pixel as a token is far too many: a $224\times224$ image has $50{,}176$ pixels, so $50{,}176^2\approx2.5$ billion attention weights per head. The Vision Transformer's answer: an image is worth 16×16 words. Cut the image into square patches, flatten each patch, and map it with one shared linear layer to a vector. Now the image is a short "sentence" of patch tokens, and a standard Transformer encoder does the rest.

Tiny numbers: ViT-Base/16 on a 224 × 224 colour image
  • Patches: $224/16=14$ per side, so $14\times14=196$ patches.
  • Each patch: $16\cdot16\cdot3=768$ numbers, mapped by one $768\times768$ matrix (+ bias) to a $D=768$ token: $590{,}592$ parameters.
  • Plus the [class] token: $197$ tokens, each with its own learned position embedding.
  • Attention weights per head per layer: $197^2=38{,}809$. With 12 heads and 12 layers: $5{,}588{,}496$ per image.
  • Halve the patch size to 8: $784+1$ tokens, $785^2=616{,}225$ weights: about $16\times$ more. Tokens grow with $1/P^2$, attention with $1/P^4$.
Formula
$$\mathbf z_0=\big[\,\mathbf x_{\text{class}};\;\mathbf x_p^1\mathbf E;\;\mathbf x_p^2\mathbf E;\;\dots;\;\mathbf x_p^N\mathbf E\,\big]+\mathbf E_{\text{pos}},\qquad N=\frac{HW}{P^2}$$

$\mathbf x_p^i\in\mathbb R^{P^2C}$: patch $i$, flattened · $\mathbf E\in\mathbb R^{P^2C\times D}$: the shared patch projection · $\mathbf x_{\text{class}}$: the learnable [class] token · $\mathbf E_{\text{pos}}\in\mathbb R^{(N+1)\times D}$: one learned position vector per token · $H,W,C$: image height, width, channels. Then $L$ Transformer blocks, and the prediction is an MLP head on the final [class] vector.

In code: patchify with reshape and permute
import torch
torch.manual_seed(0)
img = torch.randn(1, 3, 224, 224)                       # (B, C, H, W)
P = 16
B, C, H, W = img.shape
patches = img.reshape(B, C, H // P, P, W // P, P)       # cut rows and columns into blocks
patches = patches.permute(0, 2, 4, 1, 3, 5)             # (B, 14, 14, C, P, P)
patches = patches.reshape(B, (H // P) * (W // P), C * P * P)
print(patches.shape)

D = 768
W_e = torch.randn(C * P * P, D) * 0.02                  # linear projection (learned)
tokens = patches @ W_e                                  # (1, 196, 768)
cls = torch.zeros(1, 1, D)                              # learnable [class] token
pos = torch.randn(1, 197, D) * 0.02                     # learnable position embeddings
x = torch.cat([cls, tokens], dim=1) + pos
print(x.shape)

# the same projection, written as a convolution with kernel = stride = P
conv = torch.nn.functional.conv2d(img, W_e.T.reshape(D, C, P, P), stride=P)
print(torch.allclose(conv.flatten(2).transpose(1, 2), tokens, atol=1e-4))
# torch.Size([1, 196, 768])
# torch.Size([1, 197, 768])
# True

ViT versus CNN

Slide 11AnswerWhy
Similar in ViT and CNN"Convolutions at each layer (shared MLP)"The MLP in every block is one network applied to every patch with the same weights, exactly like a $1\times1$ convolution. The patch embedding itself is a convolution with kernel size and stride $P$ (the code above checks this).
Different in a ViT2D position embeddingA convolution knows where neighbours are through its kernel layout. A ViT knows nothing about the 2D grid; positions must be added. (The original ViT learns one embedding per patch index, and these learn the 2D row/column structure by themselves.)
Self-attention at each layerPatches exchange information through attention instead of a fixed kernel.
Global receptive field after SAEvery patch sees every patch in layer 1. A $3\times3$ CNN needs many layers (Week 6a).
SA is input dependentAttention weights are computed from the image; a convolution kernel is the same for every image once trained.
The price of fewer assumptions

A CNN has inductive biases built in: locality (a pixel's neighbours matter most) and translation equivariance (the same kernel everywhere, Week 6a). A ViT has almost none, so it must learn them from data. Trained on ImageNet alone (1.3M images), a ViT does a bit worse than a comparable ResNet; pre-trained on 14M–300M images it does better. That is exactly the foundation-model recipe: pre-train big, then adapt.

5Video, audio, anything: tokens slide 11

Q: How would you apply it on video? On other signals?

Video: make 3D patches, small blocks of $t$ frames × $P\times P$ pixels (often called tubelets). Each block is flattened and projected like an image patch, and the position embedding now says where the block is in space and time.

Other signals: "as long as it can be turned into patches/tokens". Audio becomes a spectrogram (time × frequency) and is cut into patches like an image; a time series into windows; a point cloud into groups of points. The Transformer after the tokenizer stays the same.

Tiny numbers: a 16-frame video clip
  • 16 frames of $224\times224$, tubelets of 2 frames × $16\times16$ pixels: $\tfrac{16}{2}\cdot196=1568$ tokens, each $2\cdot16\cdot16\cdot3=1536$ numbers.
  • Attention weights per head per layer: $1568^2=2{,}458{,}624$, which is $8^2=64\times$ the $196^2$ of one image. Video Transformers fight this cost, e.g. with attention over space and over time separately.

6Pre-training without labels slide 13 He et al. 2021 (MAE)

Q: What objective do foundation models use to train on large texts?

A: next-word prediction. Let the model learn to reconstruct its input: at every position, predict the token that comes next. The targets are just the text shifted by one place, so no additional human ground-truth labels are needed. That is self-supervised learning, and it is why the whole internet can be training data. To predict the next word well, a model has to pick up grammar, facts and some reasoning on the way.

Tiny numbers: one sentence, six examples

"<s> the cat sat on the mat" has 7 tokens. Inputs: the first 6; targets: the last 6. One forward pass with a causal mask (Week 7a) gives 6 predictions at once, each scored with cross-entropy. With random scores over a vocabulary of 6 words, the loss is about $\ln6\approx1.79$ or worse; the snippet below gets 2.298.

In code: next-token targets and the loss
import torch
torch.manual_seed(0)
vocab = ["<s>", "the", "cat", "sat", "on", "mat"]
ids = torch.tensor([0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 1, 5])         # <s> the cat sat on the mat
inputs, targets = ids[:-1], ids[1:]                # targets = inputs shifted by one
for i, t in zip(inputs.tolist(), targets.tolist()):
    print(f"{vocab[i]:>4} -> {vocab[t]}")
logits = torch.randn(len(inputs), len(vocab))      # the model's scores (random here)
logp = logits - logits.logsumexp(dim=1, keepdim=True)
loss = -logp[torch.arange(len(targets)), targets].mean()
print(round(loss.item(), 3))
#  <s> -> the
#  the -> cat
#  cat -> sat
#  sat -> on
#   on -> the
#  the -> mat
# 2.298

A second text objective you may meet: BERT hides 15% of the words and predicts them from both sides (masked language modelling). It is also self-supervised; it trains an encoder instead of a generator.

Q: How would you do that for images? A: the masked autoencoder

Intuition

Text has a natural "next" token; images don't. So hide parts of the image instead and ask the model to fill them in. This is the denoising-autoencoder idea of Week 7b, with "noise" = removing patches. Two design choices make the MAE work:

  • Mask a lot: 75%. Images are very redundant: a single missing patch can be guessed from its neighbours with no understanding at all. Hiding three quarters removes whole regions, so the model has to know what things look like. (Text is denser in meaning, so BERT masks only 15%.) The widget below shows this.
  • The encoder sees only the visible patches. No mask tokens go into the big encoder; they are added only for the small decoder. With 25% of the patches, the encoder's attention is $16\times$ cheaper, and pre-training is several times faster. At fine-tuning time the encoder sees whole images and the decoder is thrown away.
Tiny numbers: MAE on 196 patches
  • Masked: $0.75\cdot196=147$ patches; visible: $49$.
  • Encoder attention: $49^2=2{,}401$ weights instead of $196^2=38{,}416$: $16\times$ fewer, since $(1/0.25)^2=16$.
  • The loss is the mean squared error over the 147 hidden patches only. The 49 visible ones would be trivial to copy.
Formula
$$\mathcal L_{\text{MAE}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal M|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal M}\frac{1}{P^2C}\big\|\hat{\mathbf x}_i-\mathbf x_i\big\|^2$$

$\mathcal M$: the set of masked patches · $\mathbf x_i$: the true pixels of patch $i$ · $\hat{\mathbf x}_i$: the decoder's prediction · $P^2C$: numbers per patch.

In code: random masking as in the MAE code
import torch
torch.manual_seed(0)
N, L, D = 1, 196, 768
x = torch.randn(N, L, D)                           # patch tokens (+ positions)
mask_ratio = 0.75
len_keep = int(L * (1 - mask_ratio))               # 49 patches stay visible
noise = torch.rand(N, L)
ids_shuffle = noise.argsort(dim=1)                 # a random order per image
ids_keep = ids_shuffle[:, :len_keep]
x_visible = torch.gather(x, 1, ids_keep[..., None].expand(-1, -1, D))
mask = torch.ones(N, L)
mask[:, :len_keep] = 0
mask = torch.gather(mask, 1, ids_shuffle.argsort(dim=1))   # back to image order: 1 = masked
print(x_visible.shape, int(mask.sum()))

pred, target = torch.randn(N, L, 768), torch.randn(N, L, 768)
per_patch = ((pred - target) ** 2).mean(dim=-1)    # (N, L): error per patch
loss = (per_patch * mask).sum() / mask.sum()       # only the masked patches count
print(round(loss.item(), 3))
# torch.Size([1, 49, 768]) 147
# 2.01

The loss is about 2 because the prediction and the target are independent standard normals here: $\mathbb E[(a-b)^2]=1+1$.

7Images and captions: CLIP slide 14 Radford et al. 2021

Q: Given N images with their N captions, how to pre-train?

The internet is full of images with a sentence next to them. CLIP trains two encoders: one turns an image into a vector, the other turns a caption into a vector of the same size. Training goal, straight from slide 14:

  • Maximise the cosine similarity of the $N$ correct pairs (image $i$ with caption $i$);
  • Minimise the cosine similarity of the $N^2-N$ incorrect pairs.

This is contrastive learning: no class labels, just "these two belong together, those don't". CLIP was trained on 400 million image–text pairs with batches of $N=32{,}768$.

Formula
$$s_{ij}=\frac{\mathbf I_i^\top\mathbf T_j}{\|\mathbf I_i\|\,\|\mathbf T_j\|},\qquad \mathcal L=\frac12\Bigg[\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N-\log\frac{e^{s_{ii}/\tau}}{\sum_{j}e^{s_{ij}/\tau}}\;+\;\frac1N\sum_{j=1}^N-\log\frac{e^{s_{jj}/\tau}}{\sum_{i}e^{s_{ij}/\tau}}\Bigg]$$

$\mathbf I_i,\mathbf T_j$: image and text embeddings · $s_{ij}$: their cosine similarity · $\tau$: the temperature, learned (it starts at 0.07); a small $\tau$ makes the softmax sharper · first sum: for each image, cross-entropy over all captions (rows); second sum: for each caption, over all images (columns). It is a classification problem with $N$ classes where the correct "class" is the diagonal.

Tiny numbers: two pairs

Similarities $\begin{bmatrix}0.8&0.2\\0.4&0.6\end{bmatrix}$, $\tau=0.1$, so the scores are $\begin{bmatrix}8&2\\4&6\end{bmatrix}$. With two options, $-\log\frac{e^a}{e^a+e^b}=\log(1+e^{-(a-b)})$:

  • Rows: $\log(1+e^{-6})=0.0025$ and $\log(1+e^{-2})=0.127$; mean $0.065$.
  • Columns: $\log(1+e^{-4})=0.018$ twice; mean $0.018$.
  • $\mathcal L=(0.065+0.018)/2=0.041$. With $\tau=1$ the same similarities give $0.515$: the temperature decides how hard the loss pushes.
In code: the symmetric contrastive loss and a zero-shot guess
import torch
torch.manual_seed(0)
N, D = 4, 8
img = torch.randn(N, D)                            # image-encoder outputs
txt = img + 0.8 * torch.randn(N, D)                # text-encoder outputs, roughly paired
img = img / img.norm(dim=1, keepdim=True)          # L2-normalise: dot product = cosine
txt = txt / txt.norm(dim=1, keepdim=True)
S = img @ txt.T                                    # (N, N) cosine similarities
tau = 0.07
logits = S / tau
labels = torch.arange(N)                           # image i belongs with caption i

def ce(z, y):                                      # cross-entropy, one row per example
    return (z.logsumexp(dim=1) - z[torch.arange(len(y)), y]).mean()

loss = (ce(logits, labels) + ce(logits.T, labels)) / 2   # image→text and text→image
print(S.round(decimals=2))
print(round(loss.item(), 3))

classes = ["plane", "car", "dog", "bird"]          # zero-shot: captions as a classifier
print(classes[(txt @ img[2]).argmax()])
# tensor([[ 0.9500,  0.5800, -0.1600,  0.0400],
#         [ 0.3500,  0.6700, -0.1400,  0.5600],
#         [-0.0300, -0.5000,  0.9300, -0.1200],
#         [ 0.5000,  0.7900, -0.4600,  0.4700]])
# 1.045

Zero-shot classification (slide 14, panels 2 and 3)
  1. Put each class name into a sentence: "A photo of a {object}." and encode all of them with the text encoder.
  2. Encode the new image with the image encoder.
  3. Predict the class whose sentence has the highest cosine similarity with the image.

No training images of these classes are needed: the "classifier weights" are text embeddings. Zero-shot CLIP matched a ResNet-50 trained on ImageNet's 1.28 million labelled images, on ImageNet itself (76.2% top-1), and it is far more robust to other image styles (sketches, renderings).

(2) At 1, raise τ to 1: the embeddings are perfect, but the loss stays high, because scores between $-1$ and $1$ only give a soft softmax. That is why CLIP learns a small $\tau$. (3) With $N=6$ in 2 dimensions the wrong pairs can't all reach $-1$: "minimise" really means "make them lower than the correct pair".

8Adapting: fine-tuning and PEFT slide 16 Han et al. 2024 (PEFT survey)

Q: How to fine-tune on new data? (slide 16)

Full fine-tuning continues training all weights on the new task's data. That works, but for a big model it is expensive: you need the memory to train every weight (plus the optimiser state, e.g. Adam's two running averages per weight, Week 4a); every task needs its own full copy (GPT-3: 175 billion numbers per copy); and with little data the many free weights overfit or forget what pre-training taught them.

Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) keeps the pre-trained parameters frozen (blue on the slide) and trains only a few parameters (red). The frozen model is shared by all tasks; a task is just its few red parameters.

Formula: the adapter
$$\operatorname{Adapter}(\mathbf x)=\mathbf W_{\text{up}}\,\sigma(\mathbf W_{\text{down}}\mathbf x)+\mathbf x,\qquad \mathbf W_{\text{down}}\in\mathbb R^{m\times d},\;\mathbf W_{\text{up}}\in\mathbb R^{d\times m},\;m\ll d$$

$\mathbf x$: a token vector of size $d$ · $m$: the bottleneck size · $\sigma$: a non-linearity (ReLU) · $+\mathbf x$: a skip connection, so with small $\mathbf W_{\text{up}}$ the adapter starts close to the identity and doesn't disturb the pre-trained model. Parameters: $2md$ (+ $m+d$ biases). In the original design (Houlsby et al. 2019) there are two adapters per block, after attention and after the MLP; with 3.6% extra parameters per task they came within 0.4% of full fine-tuning on the GLUE language benchmark.

9LoRA up close slide 16 Hu et al. 2021 past exam

Intuition

Fine-tuning changes a weight matrix: $\mathbf W_0\to\mathbf W_0+\Delta\mathbf W$. Full fine-tuning learns all $d\cdot k$ numbers of $\Delta\mathbf W$. The LoRA bet: the change needed for one task is simple, so it can be written as a product of two thin matrices, $\Delta\mathbf W=\mathbf B\mathbf A$, with a small inner size $r$. You freeze $\mathbf W_0$ and learn only $\mathbf A$ and $\mathbf B$: $r(d+k)$ numbers instead of $dk$.

In the network, $\mathbf B\mathbf A\mathbf x$ is a second path next to the frozen one: squeeze the input down to $r$ numbers with $\mathbf A$, expand back to $d$ with $\mathbf B$, and add the result to $\mathbf W_0\mathbf x$. It replaces nothing and inserts no new layer: it re-projects an existing matrix.

Formula (slide 16)
$$\mathbf h=\mathbf W_0\mathbf x+\Delta\mathbf W\mathbf x=\mathbf W_0\mathbf x+\mathbf B\mathbf A\mathbf x,\qquad \mathbf W_0\in\mathbb R^{d\times k},\;\mathbf A\in\mathbb R^{r\times k},\;\mathbf B\in\mathbb R^{d\times r},\;r\ll\min(d,k)$$

$\mathbf x\in\mathbb R^k$: the layer's input · $\mathbf h\in\mathbb R^d$: its output · $\mathbf W_0$: the pre-trained weight, frozen · $\mathbf A,\mathbf B$: trainable · $r$: the rank. Because $\mathbf B\mathbf A$ passes through $r$ numbers, its rank is at most $r$.

  • Initialisation: $\mathbf A$ random (small Gaussian), $\mathbf B=\mathbf 0$. Then $\mathbf B\mathbf A=\mathbf 0$ at the start: the model begins exactly as the pre-trained one. (If both were zero, neither would ever get a gradient.)
  • Scaling: the LoRA paper multiplies $\mathbf B\mathbf A\mathbf x$ by $\alpha/r$, so changing $r$ doesn't require re-tuning the learning rate.
  • Where: the paper puts it on the attention projections $\mathbf W_q$ and $\mathbf W_v$; it can go on any weight matrix.
  • Merging: after training, store $\mathbf W=\mathbf W_0+\mathbf B\mathbf A$. Same shape, same speed as the original model. To switch tasks, subtract one $\mathbf B\mathbf A$ and add another.
  • Scale: on GPT-3 (175B parameters), LoRA cut the trainable parameters by about $10{,}000\times$ and the GPU memory for training by $3\times$, while matching full fine-tuning.
Tiny numbers: a rank-1 update by hand

$d=k=3$, $r=1$, $\mathbf W_0=\mathbf I$, $\mathbf B=(1,2,0)^\top$, $\mathbf A=(0.5,\;0,\;-1)$, $\mathbf x=(2,1,0)^\top$.

  • $\mathbf A\mathbf x=0.5\cdot2+0+0=1$: squeezed to one number.
  • $\mathbf B(\mathbf A\mathbf x)=(1,2,0)^\top$: expanded back.
  • $\mathbf h=\mathbf W_0\mathbf x+\mathbf B\mathbf A\mathbf x=(2,1,0)+(1,2,0)=(3,3,0)$.
  • $\Delta\mathbf W=\mathbf B\mathbf A=\begin{bmatrix}0.5&0&-1\\1&0&-2\\0&0&0\end{bmatrix}$: every row is a multiple of $\mathbf A$, so rank 1. Trainable: $3+3=6$ numbers instead of 9.
  • Realistic: $d=k=4096$, $r=8$: $8\cdot8192=65{,}536$ instead of $16{,}777{,}216$, i.e. 0.39%.

In code: a LoRA layer, forward and backward without autograd (course convention)

The course's code writes y = x @ W with W of shape (D_in, D_out). So here W0$=\mathbf W_0^\top$, A has shape (D_in, r) $=\mathbf A^\top$ and B has shape (r, D_out) $=\mathbf B^\top$, and $\mathbf B\mathbf A\mathbf x$ becomes x @ A @ B.

import torch
torch.manual_seed(0)
N, D_in, D_out, r = 5, 64, 32, 2
W0 = torch.randn(D_in, D_out)          # pre-trained weight, frozen
A = torch.randn(D_in, r) * 0.01        # trainable: small random numbers
B = torch.zeros(r, D_out)              # trainable: zero, so A @ B = 0 at the start
x = torch.randn(N, D_in)

# forward: the frozen path plus the low-rank path
z = x @ A                              # (N, r): squeeze down to rank r
y = x @ W0 + z @ B                     # (N, D_out)
print(torch.allclose(y, x @ W0))       # at the start LoRA changes nothing

# backward (no autograd): only A and B get gradients
dy = torch.randn(N, D_out)             # upstream gradient dL/dy
dB = z.T @ dy                          # (r, N) @ (N, D_out) = (r, D_out)
dA = x.T @ (dy @ B.T)                  # (D_in, N) @ (N, r) = (D_in, r)
dx = dy @ W0.T + (dy @ B.T) @ A.T      # (N, D_in): passed on to earlier layers
print(dA.abs().max().item(), dB.shape) # dA is 0 in the first step because B = 0

print(D_in * D_out, r * (D_in + D_out))  # full update vs LoRA: trainable numbers
W = W0 + A @ B                         # after training: merge into one matrix
print(torch.allclose(x @ W, x @ W0 + (x @ A) @ B))

The gradients follow the usual linear-layer rules (Week 3b), applied twice: $\mathbf y=\mathbf z\mathbf B$ gives $\bar{\mathbf B}=\mathbf z^\top\bar{\mathbf y}$ and $\bar{\mathbf z}=\bar{\mathbf y}\mathbf B^\top$; $\mathbf z=\mathbf x\mathbf A$ gives $\bar{\mathbf A}=\mathbf x^\top\bar{\mathbf z}$. In the first step $\mathbf B=\mathbf 0$, so $\bar{\mathbf z}=\mathbf 0$ and only $\mathbf B$ moves; from then on both learn. Checked against autograd in E4.

The 2025 exam question (you missed it)

"Which statement best describes LoRA?" The four options are exactly the four red boxes of slide 16, so learn the mapping:

✓"LoRA learns a smaller amount of parameters that are projected back on existing parameters." → reparameterized: $\mathbf W_0+\mathbf B\mathbf A$.
✗"LoRA adds a set of learnable input tokens." → that is prompt tuning (additive).
✗"LoRA replaces and learns a subset of existing parameters." → that is selective fine-tuning. LoRA replaces nothing; $\mathbf W_0$ stays.
✗"LoRA injects a small number of new learnable parameters between existing layers." → that is an adapter (additive). LoRA runs in parallel with a matrix, not between layers.

10Recap

What to know for the exam (slide 18)
  • Foundation models: large models, broadly pre-trained, adapted to many downstream tasks. Not technically new; the scale is. Emergence (induced, not constructed) and homogenization (one model for many tasks). Social impact: fairness, economics, environment, misuse, legality, ethics.
  • Transformer architecture: the homogenization of models. Learnable parameters: embeddings, $\mathbf W_q,\mathbf W_k,\mathbf W_v,\mathbf W_o$, MLPs, norms, output layer.
  • Other modalities: images as patches (ViT: similar to a CNN in the shared per-token MLP; different in positions, self-attention, global and input-dependent mixing), video as 3D patches, anything that can be tokenized.
  • Pre-training: self-supervised. Text: next-word prediction. Images: masked autoencoder (mask 75%, encoder on visible patches only, loss on masked patches). Images + captions: CLIP (maximise $N$ correct similarities, minimise $N^2-N$ incorrect ones; zero-shot via prompts).
  • PEFT: freeze the model, train few parameters. Selective/substitute (train a subset, e.g. the last layer), additive/inject (adapter $\mathbf W_{\text{up}}\sigma(\mathbf W_{\text{down}}\mathbf x)+\mathbf x$; prompt tuning), reparameterized/reproject (LoRA: $\mathbf h=\mathbf W_0\mathbf x+\mathbf B\mathbf A\mathbf x$, $r\ll\min(d,k)$).

11Handwriting blueprint

Write each step on paper first, then reveal it.

1 · Tokens and shapes of ViT-Base/16

  1. Image $224\times224\times3$, $P=16$: $224/16=14$ patches per side, $N=196$.
  2. Each patch flattens to $16\cdot16\cdot3=768$ numbers. Projection $\mathbf E$: $768\times768$ plus bias $=590{,}592$ parameters.
  3. Prepend [class]: $197$ tokens of size $768$; add $197$ learned position vectors.
  4. Attention weights per head per layer: $197^2=38{,}809$. The prediction comes from an MLP head on the final [class] vector.

2 · Tokens for a video

  1. 16 frames of $224\times224$; tubelets of $2\times16\times16$.
  2. Per frame pair: $196$ blocks; frame pairs: $16/2=8$; tokens: $8\cdot196=1568$.
  3. Numbers per token: $2\cdot16\cdot16\cdot3=1536$.
  4. Attention: $1568^2\approx2.46$ million weights per head per layer, $64\times$ an image's $196^2$.

3 · A CLIP loss by hand

  1. Similarities $\begin{bmatrix}0.8&0.2\\0.4&0.6\end{bmatrix}$, $\tau=0.1$: scores $\begin{bmatrix}8&2\\4&6\end{bmatrix}$.
  2. Rows (image → caption): $\log(1+e^{-(8-2)})=0.0025$, $\log(1+e^{-(6-4)})=0.127$. Mean $0.065$.
  3. Columns (caption → image): $\log(1+e^{-(8-4)})=0.018$, $\log(1+e^{-(6-2)})=0.018$. Mean $0.018$.
  4. $\mathcal L=\tfrac12(0.065+0.018)=0.041$. The weakest link is row 2: image 2's own caption scores 0.6 versus 0.4 for the wrong one.

4 · A LoRA forward pass and its parameter count

  1. $\mathbf W_0=\mathbf I_3$, $\mathbf A=(0.5,0,-1)$ ($1\times3$), $\mathbf B=(1,2,0)^\top$ ($3\times1$), $\mathbf x=(2,1,0)^\top$.
  2. $\mathbf A\mathbf x=1$, then $\mathbf B\mathbf A\mathbf x=(1,2,0)^\top$.
  3. $\mathbf h=(2,1,0)+(1,2,0)=(3,3,0)$.
  4. Count: full $dk$ versus LoRA $r(d+k)$. For $d=k=4096$, $r=8$: $16{,}777{,}216$ versus $65{,}536$ (0.39%).

5 · LoRA backward in the course's row convention

  1. Forward: $\mathbf z=\mathbf x\mathbf A$ with shapes $(N,D_{\text{in}})(D_{\text{in}},r)=(N,r)$; $\mathbf y=\mathbf x\mathbf W_0+\mathbf z\mathbf B$, $(N,D_{\text{out}})$.
  2. $\bar{\mathbf B}=\mathbf z^\top\bar{\mathbf y}$: $(r,N)(N,D_{\text{out}})=(r,D_{\text{out}})$.
  3. $\bar{\mathbf z}=\bar{\mathbf y}\mathbf B^\top$: $(N,r)$; then $\bar{\mathbf A}=\mathbf x^\top\bar{\mathbf z}$: $(D_{\text{in}},r)$.
  4. $\bar{\mathbf x}=\bar{\mathbf y}\mathbf W_0^\top+\bar{\mathbf z}\mathbf A^\top$: $(N,D_{\text{in}})$. No gradient is stored for $\mathbf W_0$: it is frozen.

6 · MAE bookkeeping

  1. 196 patches, mask ratio 0.75: $147$ masked, $49$ visible.
  2. The encoder gets the 49 visible tokens only: attention $49^2=2{,}401$ versus $196^2=38{,}416$, i.e. $16\times$ less.
  3. The decoder gets 196 tokens: 49 encoded + 147 copies of the learned mask token, all with position embeddings.
  4. Loss: mean squared error over the 147 masked patches only.

12Exam traps

Watch out
  • "Foundation models are a new kind of technique." False. Technically not new (deep networks, self-supervised learning, transfer learning); the scale and scope are (slide 3).
  • "Self-supervised means there is no loss or no target." False. There is a target; it is made from the input itself (the next word, the hidden patches).
  • "Emergence = homogenization." No. Emergence: behaviour that appears without being built in. Homogenization: the same method/architecture/model used everywhere.
  • "A ViT uses convolutions instead of attention." False. The similarity with a CNN is the shared per-token MLP (a $1\times1$ convolution) and the patch embedding (a convolution with stride $P$); the mixing between patches is self-attention.
  • "A ViT's receptive field grows layer by layer, like a CNN's." False: global after the first self-attention layer.
  • "Attention weights are fixed after training, like a kernel." False. The learned $\mathbf W_q,\mathbf W_k,\mathbf W_v$ are fixed; the attention weights are computed from each input (input dependent).
  • "A ViT beats a CNN on any dataset." False. With little data the CNN's inductive biases win; ViTs shine after large-scale pre-training.
  • Smaller patches cost a lot: tokens $\propto1/P^2$, attention $\propto1/P^4$.
  • "The MAE encoder processes the mask tokens." False. Only the visible patches; mask tokens join in the decoder.
  • "The MAE loss is over all patches." False: over the masked patches only.
  • "CLIP minimises the similarity of $N^2$ pairs." $N^2-N$: the $N$ diagonal pairs are the correct ones. And it's a softmax cross-entropy over rows and columns, not a plain sum of similarities.
  • "CLIP needs labelled classes." False. Captions from the web; classes only appear at test time, as sentences (zero-shot).
  • "LoRA replaces / inserts / adds tokens." None of these (2025 MC 15, missed): it learns a low-rank $\mathbf B\mathbf A$ added to an existing frozen matrix.
  • "LoRA makes inference slower." False: merge $\mathbf W_0+\mathbf B\mathbf A$. Adapters do add layers; prompt tuning lengthens the input.
  • "Initialise both $\mathbf A$ and $\mathbf B$ at zero." No: then both gradients are zero forever. $\mathbf B=\mathbf 0$, $\mathbf A$ random.
  • Shapes: slide: $\mathbf A\in\mathbb R^{r\times k}$, $\mathbf B\in\mathbb R^{d\times r}$, $\mathbf h=\mathbf W_0\mathbf x$. Course code: everything transposed, x @ A @ B with A (D_in, r).
  • "PEFT trains a small model instead of the big one." False. The big model is still used in every forward pass; it is only frozen.
  • "Batch norm" on slide 9 is layer norm ("not batch norm but another").

13Self-check

Answer first, then open. past exam marks questions from the exam reviews.

1. past exam Which statement best describes LoRA? (a) It learns a smaller amount of parameters that are projected back on existing parameters. (b) It adds a set of learnable input tokens. (c) It replaces and learns a subset of existing parameters. (d) It injects a small number of new learnable parameters between existing layers. (2025 MC 15, missed)
(a). (b) is prompt tuning, (c) is selective fine-tuning, (d) is an adapter. LoRA freezes $\mathbf W_0$ and learns $\mathbf B\mathbf A$, which is added to (projected back onto) the existing weight.
2. Which of these is a foundation model: a CNN trained on MNIST to read digits; CLIP; a random forest for credit scoring?
CLIP. Trained on broad data (400M image–caption pairs) and adaptable to many tasks (zero-shot classification, retrieval, as the image encoder of other systems). The other two are trained for one task.
3. Emergence or homogenization? (i) GPT-3 translates after seeing two examples in its prompt. (ii) Sentiment analysis, question answering and named-entity recognition are all done by fine-tuning the same BERT.
(i) Emergence: in-context learning was never explicitly trained. (ii) Homogenization: one model consolidated across many applications.
4. (Slide 11) What is similar in a ViT and a CNN, and what is different?
Similar: the same weights are applied at every position: the per-token MLP is like a $1\times1$ convolution (and the patch embedding is a strided convolution). Different: the ViT needs a 2D position embedding, mixes patches by self-attention at every layer, has a global receptive field after one layer, and its mixing weights depend on the input.
5. Why does a ViT need more training data than a CNN of similar size?
It lacks the CNN's inductive biases (locality, translation equivariance). It must learn from data that nearby patches are related and that a cat is a cat anywhere in the image. With little data a CNN wins; with large pre-training a ViT wins.
6. How many tokens does ViT-Base/16 make from a 224 × 224 image, and what happens to the attention cost with 8 × 8 patches?
$196$ patches + [class] $=197$. With $P=8$: $784+1=785$ tokens, and $785^2/197^2\approx16$ times as many attention weights.
7. How would you apply a Transformer to video, and what does it cost?
Cut the video into 3D patches (space × time), flatten and project each, add space-time position embeddings (slide 11). With $T$ frames and tubelets of $t$ frames, the token count grows by $T/t$ and the attention by $(T/t)^2$.
8. Why is next-word prediction called self-supervised?
The target for every position is the next token of the same text: the input provides the labels. No human annotation is needed, so any amount of raw text can be used.
9. Why does the MAE mask 75% of the patches, when BERT masks only 15% of the words? And why doesn't its encoder see the mask tokens?
Images are highly redundant: with few patches missing, they can be filled in from neighbours without understanding anything. A high ratio forces the model to learn what objects look like. The encoder sees only the visible 25%, which makes it about $16\times$ cheaper per layer; and at fine-tuning time there are no mask tokens, so the encoder never has to deal with them.
10. CLIP trains with batches of N = 32,768 image–caption pairs. How many correct and how many incorrect pairs does one batch contain?
Correct: $N=32{,}768$ (the diagonal). Incorrect: $N^2-N=1{,}073{,}741{,}824-32{,}768=1{,}073{,}709{,}056$. Large batches give many negatives for free.
11. How do you classify images into new classes with CLIP, without any training?
Write each class as a caption ("A photo of a {class}."), encode the captions and the image, and pick the class with the highest cosine similarity: zero-shot classification (slide 14).
12. LoRA initialises $\mathbf B=\mathbf 0$ and $\mathbf A$ random. Why not the other way round, or both zero? What changes at inference?
With $\mathbf B=\mathbf 0$ the update $\mathbf B\mathbf A$ is zero, so training starts from the pre-trained model; $\mathbf A$ random means $\mathbf B$ gets a non-zero gradient ($\mathbf z^\top\bar{\mathbf y}$ with $\mathbf z=\mathbf x\mathbf A\ne\mathbf 0$). $\mathbf A=\mathbf 0$, $\mathbf B$ random also starts at zero and works too; both zero gives zero gradients for both, forever. At inference: nothing changes, merge $\mathbf W=\mathbf W_0+\mathbf B\mathbf A$.
13. Fine-tuning only the last layer of a pre-trained model: which PEFT family is that, and what does it cost at test time?
Selective (replace and re-learn a subset of the existing parameters, slide 16). No extra test-time cost: the network has the same layers.
14. In prompt tuning, what exactly is trained? Is the model changed?
Only a fixed number of extra input vectors ("soft prompt" tokens), prepended to the input embeddings. Every weight of the model stays frozen. The cost: a slightly longer sequence in every forward pass.

E2 · The CLIP loss by hand

Two images and their captions have cosine similarities $\begin{bmatrix}0.8&0.2\\0.4&0.6\end{bmatrix}$ (rows: images, columns: captions). (a) Compute the CLIP loss with $\tau=0.1$. (b) And with $\tau=1$. (c) Which entry would a gradient step push down hardest? Hint

With two options, $-\log\frac{e^a}{e^a+e^b}=\log(1+e^{-(a-b)})$. Average the two row losses and the two column losses, then average those two numbers.

Show worked solution

(a) Scores $\begin{bmatrix}8&2\\4&6\end{bmatrix}$. Rows: $\log(1+e^{-6})=0.0025$ and $\log(1+e^{-2})=0.1269$, mean $0.0647$. Columns: $\log(1+e^{-4})=0.0181$ for both, mean $0.0181$. $\mathcal L=$ 0.0414.

(b) Rows: $\log(1+e^{-0.6})=0.4375$, $\log(1+e^{-0.2})=0.5981$; columns: $\log(1+e^{-0.4})=0.5130$ twice. $\mathcal L=$ 0.5154. Same embeddings, much higher loss: with $\tau=1$ a similarity gap of $0.2$ barely moves the softmax.

(c) The incorrect pair $(2,1)$ with $0.4$: it is the closest competitor of a correct pair, in row 2 (gap only 2 in score units). Its softmax weight is the largest wrong one, and the gradient of cross-entropy w.r.t. a wrong score equals its softmax probability.

(d) Cat (0.31 > 0.27). No: the classifier is just the two text embeddings, created from words. That is zero-shot classification.

E3 · Choosing and counting a PEFT method

A company has a frozen ViT-Base/16 ($85{,}798{,}656$ parameters, float32 = 4 bytes each) and 100 customers, each with its own 10-class image task and a few thousand images. (a) How much storage do 100 fully fine-tuned copies need? (b) How many parameters per customer for: head only; adapters with $m=64$ (two per block, with biases); LoRA with $r=8$ on $\mathbf W_q$ and $\mathbf W_v$; prompt tuning with 10 tokens? (Each also needs a head of $7{,}690$.) (c) Storage for 100 customers with LoRA? (d) Which options add cost at test time?

Hint

Adapter: $2md+m+d$ each, 2 per block, 12 blocks. LoRA: $r(d+k)$ per matrix, 2 matrices per block, 12 blocks, $d=k=768$.

Show worked solution

(a) One copy with a head: $85{,}806{,}346\times4$ bytes $\approx343$ MB; 100 copies $\approx$ 34.3 GB.

(b) Head only: $7{,}690$. Adapters: $24\cdot(2\cdot64\cdot768+64+768)=2{,}379{,}264$, plus head $=2{,}386{,}954$ (2.8%). LoRA: $24\cdot8\cdot1536=294{,}912$, plus head $=302{,}602$ (0.35%). Prompt tuning: $7{,}680$, plus head $=15{,}370$.

(c) LoRA: $302{,}602\times4\approx1.2$ MB per customer, $\approx121$ MB for 100, plus one shared 343 MB backbone: about 0.46 GB instead of 34 GB.

(d) Adapters (two extra layers per block) and prompt tuning (10 more tokens, so slightly more attention). Head only and LoRA (after merging) cost nothing extra. With "a few thousand images" and tasks that may differ from the pre-training data, LoRA or adapters are the usual choice; head only if the features already fit.

E4 · A LoRA linear layer from scratch (coding, no autograd)

Complete the layer in the style of the 2025 coding question: only basic tensor operations, no torch.nn, no autograd. Batches are rows, W0 has shape (D_in, D_out). forward must cache what backward needs; backward stores self.A_grad and self.B_grad (not W0: it is frozen) and returns the downstream gradient.

class LoRALinear:
    def __init__(self, W0, b0, r):
        D_in, D_out = W0.shape
        self.W0, self.b0 = W0, b0                     # pre-trained, frozen
        self.A = torch.randn(D_in, r) * 0.01          # trainable
        self.B = torch.zeros(r, D_out)                # trainable, starts at 0

    def forward(self, x):                             # x: (N, D_in)
        # TODO: cache, low-rank path, output (N, D_out)

    def backward(self, dupstream):                    # dupstream: (N, D_out)
        # TODO: self.B_grad, self.A_grad, return downstream (N, D_in)
Hint

Write the shapes first: z = x @ A is (N, r). Treat the layer as two linear layers in a row plus a frozen side path. For B: what plays the role of "the input" of the second linear layer? For A: first carry the gradient back through B.

Show worked solution
    def forward(self, x):                             # x: (N, D_in)
        self.cache = x
        self.z = x @ self.A                           # (N, r)
        return x @ self.W0 + self.b0 + self.z @ self.B

    def backward(self, dupstream):                    # dupstream: (N, D_out)
        x = self.cache
        self.B_grad = self.z.T @ dupstream            # (r, D_out)
        self.A_grad = x.T @ (dupstream @ self.B.T)    # (D_in, r)
        return dupstream @ self.W0.T + (dupstream @ self.B.T) @ self.A.T   # (N, D_in)

Tests (run): (1) with a non-zero B, the output and all three gradients equal PyTorch autograd's: True True True True. (2) Adapting a frozen $20\times10$ layer to a task whose true change has rank 2, training only A and B with SGD (learning rate 0.5), the mean squared error falls from 12.4952 (step 0) to 0.0001 (step 100) and 0.0 (step 300), with $2\cdot(20+10)=60$ trainable numbers instead of $200$.

Common mistakes: storing a gradient for W0 (it is frozen); forgetting self.b0 in the forward; x @ self.B (wrong shape: the low-rank path goes through A first); returning only dupstream @ self.W0.T (the downstream gradient flows through both paths).

E5 · The CLIP loss in code

Write clip_loss(img, txt, tau) for embeddings of shape (N, D), using only basic tensor operations: normalise, build the similarity matrix, and average the cross-entropy over rows and over columns with the diagonal as the correct class. Then predict: what is the loss when txt = img and $\tau=0.07$? And with $\tau=1$?

Hint

Cross-entropy with the correct class on the diagonal: logsumexp of each row minus the diagonal entry. The columns are the rows of the transposed matrix.

Show worked solution
def clip_loss(img, txt, tau):
    img = img / img.norm(dim=1, keepdim=True)
    txt = txt / txt.norm(dim=1, keepdim=True)
    logits = img @ txt.T / tau                        # (N, N)
    def ce(z):                                        # correct class = the diagonal
        return (z.logsumexp(dim=1) - z.diag()).mean()
    return (ce(logits) + ce(logits.T)) / 2

Tests (run, torch.manual_seed(0), random (6, 16) embeddings): clip_loss gives 6.859, the same as PyTorch's F.cross_entropy on the normalised logits. With txt = img: 0.0 at $\tau=0.07$ (every diagonal score is $1/0.07\approx14$ and dominates), but 1.017 at $\tau=1$ (scores between $-1$ and $1$ can't make the softmax confident). That is why CLIP learns a small temperature.
