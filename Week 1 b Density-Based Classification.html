Density-Based Classification

Bayes' rule needs $p(\mathbf x\mid y)$ for every class, and nobody hands you that. This lecture estimates it from data in two ways: assume a Gaussian (QDA, then LDA, then nearest mean, each with fewer parameters) or let the data speak (histogram, Parzen, k-nearest neighbours). It ends with the price every density estimate pays: the curse of dimensionality.

0Test yourself first

Answer out loud before you read on. Each answer points to the part that explains it.

1. QDA, LDA and the nearest-mean classifier all assume Gaussian classes. What does each one assume about the covariance matrices?
QDA: every class has its own $\Sigma_k$. LDA: all classes share one $\Sigma$. Nearest mean: that shared $\Sigma$ is $\sigma^2 I$ (round, equal spread in every direction). Each step removes parameters. → Parts 4–7
2. You have 3 training objects per class in a 5-dimensional feature space. Which of QDA, LDA and nearest mean can you train?
Only nearest mean. With 3 points a class covariance has rank at most 2, and even the pooled one has rank at most 4 < 5, so neither can be inverted. Means are always fine. → Part 5
3. Parzen with a very small width $h$: is that high bias or high variance?
High variance (low bias). The estimate is a spike on every training point, so it changes completely with a new sample. A large $h$ smooths everything out: high bias. → Part 10

1Big picture & key terms

Week 1a assumed you know the class densities. This lecture estimates them from training data, then plugs the estimates into Bayes' rule (a plug-in classifier):

1. NeedPriors $p(y)$ (easy: count) and class densities $p(\mathbf x\mid y)$ (hard). §2
→
2. ParametricAssume a Gaussian; estimate $\boldsymbol\mu$, $\Sigma$. QDA → LDA → nearest mean. §3–8
→
3. Non-parametricNo shape assumed: histogram, Parzen, k-NN. One knob: $h$ or $k$. §9–12
→
4. The catchDensities need data that grows exponentially with the number of features. §13
→
5. AlternativeSkip the density; model the boundary directly (discriminative, Week 2b). §14
One sentence to remember

Every density-based classifier is "estimate $p(\mathbf x\mid y)$, multiply by $p(y)$, pick the largest". They differ only in how much they assume, and the less data you have, the more you should assume.

2Why we need densities, and two surprises slides 2–10

Recap of Week 1a: assign $\mathbf x$ to the class with the largest posterior, built with Bayes' rule from three ingredients (slide 10):

$$\hat p(y\mid\mathbf x)=\frac{\hat p(\mathbf x\mid y)\,\hat p(y)}{\hat p(\mathbf x)},\qquad \hat p(y_i)=\frac{n_i}{n},\qquad \hat p(\mathbf x)=\sum_{i}\hat p(\mathbf x\mid y_i)\,\hat p(y_i).$$

The priors are easy (count: $n_i$ objects of class $i$ out of $n$), $\hat p(\mathbf x)$ follows from the others, so everything hinges on $\hat p(\mathbf x\mid y)$. Slide 4's notation note: strictly, priors and posteriors are probabilities $P(\cdot)$ and only $p(\mathbf x\mid y)$ is a density; the slides write $p$ for all of them.

3The Gaussian density slides 11–16 Bishop §2.3

Intuition

A Gaussian is a "blob": dense in the middle, fading in every direction. The mean $\boldsymbol\mu$ says where the blob is; the covariance $\Sigma$ says how wide it is and how it is tilted. Lines of equal density are ellipses.

Formula
$$p(\mathbf x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{(2\pi)^p\det\Sigma}}\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac12(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)^T\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)\Big),\qquad \Sigma=\begin{bmatrix}\sigma_1^2&\rho_{12}\sigma_1\sigma_2\\ \rho_{12}\sigma_1\sigma_2&\sigma_2^2\end{bmatrix}$$

$p$: number of features · $\sigma_j^2$: variance of feature $j$ · $\rho_{12}$: correlation between features 1 and 2 · the exponent is $-\tfrac12$ times the squared Mahalanobis distance. (Week1b's deck had $2\pi^p$ under the root on slides 12–16; the week2a copy fixes it to $(2\pi)^p$.)

Tiny numbers

The peak height is $p(\boldsymbol\mu)=\frac{1}{2\pi\sqrt{\det\Sigma}}$ in 2-D. $\Sigma=I$: $\det=1$, peak $0.159$. $\Sigma=\mathrm{diag}(4,1)$: $\det=4$, peak $0.080$ (twice as spread, half as high). $\Sigma=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\1&1\end{bmatrix}$: $\det=2\cdot1-1\cdot1=1$, peak $0.159$ again: tilting and stretching can keep the area of the ellipse the same.

Estimating the parameters (slide 16)

Maximum-likelihood estimates from the $N$ training objects of one class:

$$\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf x_i,\qquad \hat\Sigma=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\mathbf x_i-\hat{\boldsymbol\mu})(\mathbf x_i-\hat{\boldsymbol\mu})^T\quad(\text{a sum of outer products})$$

4The quadratic classifier (QDA) slides 17–23

Intuition

Fit one Gaussian per class, each with its own shape (slide 17), and give every $\mathbf x$ to the class whose prior-weighted Gaussian is highest. Because the shapes differ, the line where two of them are equal is curved: an ellipse, parabola or hyperbola.

Formula

Take the log of the posterior and drop what is the same for every class ($-\frac p2\log2\pi$ and $-\log p(\mathbf x)$) (slides 19–20):

$$g_i(\mathbf x)=-\tfrac12\log\det\Sigma_i-\tfrac12(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu_i)^T\Sigma_i^{-1}(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu_i)+\log p(y_i)\qquad\text{assign to the largest }g_i.$$

For two classes, $f(\mathbf x)=g_1(\mathbf x)-g_2(\mathbf x)$ is quadratic in $\mathbf x$ (slide 21):

$$f(\mathbf x)=\mathbf x^TW\mathbf x+\mathbf w^T\mathbf x+w_0,\quad W=\tfrac12\big(\Sigma_2^{-1}-\Sigma_1^{-1}\big),\quad \mathbf w=\Sigma_1^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu_1-\Sigma_2^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu_2,$$ $$w_0=-\tfrac12\log\det\Sigma_1-\tfrac12\boldsymbol\mu_1^T\Sigma_1^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu_1+\log p(y_1)+\tfrac12\log\det\Sigma_2+\tfrac12\boldsymbol\mu_2^T\Sigma_2^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu_2-\log p(y_2).$$

Say $y_1$ when $f(\mathbf x)>0$. The slide says "no need to remember, but try the derivation": it's blueprint 1 below.

What the boundary looks like

Slide 22 shows QDA on the banana set (better than a straight line, but a Gaussian can't bend like a banana). Slide 23 shows two Gaussians tilted in different directions: the boundary is a hyperbola, with two branches. In 1-D you saw the same effect in Week 1a: unequal variances give two boundary points.

5Estimating Σ, and when it fails slides 24–28

Tiny numbers: the slides' own example (slides 24–27)

Data $X=\{(-1,-1),(-1,1),(2,0),(3,0)\}$ with labels $y=(-1,-1,+1,+1)$. For class $+1$:

  1. Mean: $\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}=\tfrac12\big((2,0)+(3,0)\big)=(2.5,\,0)$.
  2. Centre the points: $\tilde{\mathbf x}_1=(-0.5,\,0)$, $\tilde{\mathbf x}_2=(0.5,\,0)$.
  3. Outer products: $\tilde{\mathbf x}_1\tilde{\mathbf x}_1^T=\tilde{\mathbf x}_2\tilde{\mathbf x}_2^T=\begin{bmatrix}0.25&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix}$.
  4. Average: $\hat\Sigma=\tfrac12(\cdot+\cdot)=\begin{bmatrix}0.25&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix}$, so $\det\hat\Sigma=0$.

The variance in $x_2$ is 0: no inverse ("Boohoo", slide 27). The Gaussian isn't defined, so QDA can't be used. Class $-1$ has the same problem in the other direction: $\hat\Sigma=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}$.

The rule behind it

$N$ points span at most $N-1$ directions around their mean, so $\hat\Sigma$ has rank at most $N-1$. It can only be inverted when $N_k\ge p+1$ for every class (in practice: a lot more).

Fix 1 (slide 28): average the class covariances, $\hat\Sigma=\frac1C\sum_{k=1}^{C}\hat\Sigma_k$, which uses all the data for one matrix. Here: $\tfrac12\Big(\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}0.25&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix}\Big)=\begin{bmatrix}0.125&0\\0&0.5\end{bmatrix}$, which can be inverted. That is exactly LDA (next part).

Fix 2 (Week 5a): regularise, $\hat\Sigma+\lambda I$, adding a little variance in every direction.

6The linear discriminant (LDA) slides 28–30

Intuition

Pretend all classes have the same shape: one shared $\Sigma$, estimated from all the data. Two Gaussians with the same shape are equally high along a straight line (in higher dimensions: a hyperplane), because the curved part $\mathbf x^T\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf x$ is the same for both classes and cancels.

Formula
$$f(\mathbf x)=\log p(y_1\mid\mathbf x)-\log p(y_2\mid\mathbf x)=\mathbf w^T\mathbf x+w_0,\qquad \mathbf w=\hat\Sigma^{-1}(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}_1-\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}_2),$$ $$w_0=\tfrac12\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}_2^T\hat\Sigma^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}_2-\tfrac12\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}_1^T\hat\Sigma^{-1}\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}_1+\log\frac{p(y_1)}{p(y_2)}.$$

Set $\Sigma_1=\Sigma_2$ in QDA's formula: $W=0$ and this is what's left.

Slide 29 has a sign slip

Slide 29 writes $\mathbf w=\hat\Sigma^{-1}(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}_2-\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}_1)$. With $f=\log p(y_1\mid\mathbf x)-\log p(y_2\mid\mathbf x)$ (positive means $y_1$) it must be $\hat\Sigma^{-1}(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}_1-\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}_2)$: that's what slide 21 gives with equal covariances, and what slide 32 gives for the nearest mean ($\mathbf w=\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}_1-\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}_2$). Its $w_0$ is correct. With the slide's sign, $f>0$ would point to class 2. In an exam, derive it or state which class $f>0$ means.

Tiny numbers: LDA on the slide-24 data

Pooled $\hat\Sigma=\mathrm{diag}(0.125,\,0.5)$, so $\hat\Sigma^{-1}=\mathrm{diag}(8,\,2)$. Means $\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}_{-1}=(-1,0)$, $\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}_{+1}=(2.5,0)$. With class $-1$ as $y_1$: $\mathbf w=\mathrm{diag}(8,2)(-3.5,\,0)^T=(-28,\,0)$ and $w_0=\tfrac12\cdot8\cdot2.5^2-\tfrac12\cdot8\cdot1^2=21$. Boundary: $-28x_1+21=0$, so $\mathbf{x_1=0.75}$, the vertical line halfway between the means.

Slide 30 shows LDA on the banana set: a straight, tilted line. It can't follow the bananas, but it needs far fewer numbers than QDA.

7The nearest-mean classifier slides 31–33

Intuition

Even one shared covariance can be too much to estimate. Assume every feature has the same variance and no correlation: $\Sigma=\sigma^2I$, round blobs of equal size. Then "most likely class" becomes "closest class mean", in ordinary Euclidean distance.

Formula
$$g_i(\mathbf x)=-\frac1{\sigma^2}\Big(\tfrac12\boldsymbol\mu_i^T\boldsymbol\mu_i-\boldsymbol\mu_i^T\mathbf x\Big)+\log p(y_i)\quad(\text{slide 31}),\qquad \mathbf w=\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}_1-\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}_2,\quad w_0=\tfrac12\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}_2^T\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}_2-\tfrac12\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}_1^T\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}_1+\sigma^2\log\frac{p(y_1)}{p(y_2)}.$$

With equal priors the boundary is the perpendicular bisector of the two means. Unequal priors shift it towards the rarer class.

    w.train(a)

8Which one when? Parameters versus data

This answers the question in your lecture notes: "why do we use QDA vs LDA vs NMC, something to do with less availability of data". Yes: it's about how many numbers each model must estimate from how many objects.

ModelNumbers to estimate (2 classes, $p$ features)$p=2$$p=10$$p=100$
QDA2 means + 2 covariances: $2\big(p+\frac{p(p+1)}2\big)$1013010 300
LDA2 means + 1 shared covariance: $2p+\frac{p(p+1)}2$7755 250

The rule

Few training objects: every estimated number is noisy, so the model with the fewest numbers wins even if its assumptions are a bit wrong. Many objects: the noise averages out and the most flexible model whose assumptions fit wins. That is the bias–variance trade-off you'll formalise in Week 2b and Week 5.

9Non-parametric: the histogram slides 34–40 Bishop §2.5

Real classes are often not one blob: bananas, rings, several clusters (slide 35). Then any Gaussian model is wrong however much data you have. Non-parametric methods assume no shape: the estimate is built from the training points directly.

Formula (slide 39)
$$\hat p(\mathbf x)\approx\frac1h\,\frac{k_N}{N}\quad\text{for }\mathbf x\text{ in a bin of width }h\text{ that contains }k_N\text{ of the }N\text{ objects.}$$

"Fraction of the points, divided by the volume of the bin" (in $D$ dimensions the volume is $h^D$).

  • $h$ too large: the estimate is blocky and misses detail (imprecise).
  • $h$ too small: most bins hold 0 or 1 points, so the estimate jumps around (unstable).
  • The best $h$ depends on $N$; and even the offset of the bin edges changes the picture (slide 40).

Two smoother relatives fix the edge problem: Parzen fixes the volume and counts points; k-NN fixes the count and measures the volume.

10Parzen density estimation and the Parzen classifier slides 41–45

Intuition

Put a small bump (a kernel) on every training point and add them up. Where many points are close together the bumps pile up: high density. The width $h$ of the bumps is the only knob.

Formula
$$\hat p(\mathbf z\mid h)=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}K\big(\|\mathbf z-\mathbf x_i\|,\,h\big),\qquad\text{uniform kernel: }K(r,h)=\begin{cases}1/V&r\le h\\0&r>h\end{cases}\quad(\text{slide 41})$$

Parzen classifier (slide 42): one Parzen density per class with Gaussian kernels, $\hat p(\mathbf x\mid\omega_i)=\frac1{n_i}\sum_{j=1}^{n_i}\mathcal N\big(\mathbf x\mid\mathbf x_j^{(i)},h^2I\big)$, then Bayes' rule. (The slide writes the covariance as $hI$; in PRTools $h$ is the kernel's standard deviation.)

  • Small $h$: a spike on every point; islands around single objects (slide 43, "overfitting"). Low bias, high variance.
  • Large $h$: everything smoothed into one blob; the classes blur. High bias, low variance.
  • Choosing $h$ (slide 45): the average distance to the 10th nearest neighbour; the rule of thumb $h=\sigma\big(\frac4{p+2}\big)^{\frac1{p+4}}n^{-\frac1{p+4}}$ with $\sigma^2=\frac1p\sum_i s_{ii}$ (average feature variance); or maximise the leave-one-out likelihood $\prod_i\hat p(\mathbf x_i)$, where each $\hat p(\mathbf x_i)$ is computed without $\mathbf x_i$ itself.
Tiny numbers

Rule of thumb for 1-D data ($p=1$) with $n=20$ points and $\sigma=1$: $h=1\cdot\big(\tfrac43\big)^{1/5}\cdot20^{-1/5}=1.059\times0.549\approx\mathbf{0.58}$. In Exercise 4.2 the test likelihood peaks at $h=0.5$: close.

Why "leave-one-out"?

If $\mathbf x_i$ is in its own estimate, its kernel contributes $\frac1n\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}h}$, which goes to $\infty$ as $h\to0$. So the plain training likelihood always prefers $h\to0$: the overfitted spikes. Exercise 4.1 shows exactly this.

11k-nearest neighbours: density and classifier slides 46–51

Intuition

Turn Parzen around: instead of a fixed window that counts points, grow a ball around $\mathbf x$ until it holds $k$ points. A small ball means dense, a big ball means sparse. For classification, look at which classes those $k$ neighbours belong to and take a vote.

Formula

Density: $\hat p(\mathbf x)=\dfrac{k}{n\,V_k}$, with $V_k$ the volume of the ball around $\mathbf x$ that reaches the $k$-th neighbour (slide 47).

Classifier (slide 49): of the $k$ neighbours, $k_m$ belong to class $y_m$, so

$$\hat p(\mathbf x\mid y_m)=\frac{k_m}{n_mV_k},\quad \hat p(y_m)=\frac{n_m}{n}\quad\Rightarrow\quad \hat p(\mathbf x\mid y_m)\,\hat p(y_m)=\frac{k_m}{nV_k},\qquad \hat p(y_m\mid\mathbf x)=\frac{k_m}{k}.$$

The same $V_k$ and $n$ appear for every class, so Bayes' rule reduces to majority vote: pick the class with the largest $k_m$.

The choice of k (slides 50–51)

  • $k=1$: the most ragged boundary; every training object is classified correctly by itself (training error 0).
  • Larger $k$: smoother boundary; the test error first goes down, reaches a minimum at some optimal $k$, then rises.
  • $k=n$: every object's neighbours are the whole training set, so it always predicts the largest class: $\lim_{k\to n}\varepsilon_{k\text{-NN}}=\min\big(p(y_1),p(y_2)\big)$.
Extra fact worth knowing

With infinitely many training objects, the 1-NN error is at most twice the Bayes error (Cover & Hart): $\varepsilon^*\le\varepsilon_{1\text{-NN}}\le2\varepsilon^*$.

12Scale your features slides 52–54

Intuition

Parzen and k-NN only see Euclidean distances. If one feature is measured in a unit with a much larger range (or is just noisy and wide), it dominates every distance and the useful feature is ignored. Standardise each feature (subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation) before you use them.

In code
# PRTools (slides 52-53): fit the scaling on the training set, then chain it with the classifier

Non-parametric classifiers (slide 54)
AdvantagesSimple and flexible; often very good performance; easy to tune the complexity ($h$ or $k$).
DisadvantagesNeed relatively large training sets; must store the complete training set; must compute distances to all training objects for every prediction; features must be scaled sensibly; $h$ or $k$ has to be optimised.
Who cares about scaling?

Parzen, k-NN and nearest mean all use Euclidean distance, so they are sensitive to feature scaling. LDA and QDA use $\Sigma^{-1}$, which undoes any rescaling, so with enough data their boundaries don't change (24/25 exam question). Fisher (Week 2b) is scale-invariant too; the SVM (Week 5a) is not.

13The curse of dimensionality slides 55–60 Bishop §1.4

Intuition

"More features give more information, so use more features!" Really? Each extra feature multiplies the space you have to fill with examples. With a fixed training set, the space empties out: densities become impossible to estimate and noise features drown the useful ones (slides 55–58).

Tiny numbers

A histogram with 10 bins per feature and $D=4$ features has $10^4=10\,000$ cells. With 200 training objects at least 98% of the cells are empty, so the density estimate there is 0.

What practitioners do (slide 60): use all information without overfitting; reduce the features; simplify the models (nearest mean instead of QDA); optimise carefully. See also the shell-volume figure in Week 1a and your data-sparsity animation.

14Generative vs discriminative slides 61–66 Bishop §4.2

Estimating densities is hard, especially in many dimensions. The alternative (slide 61): pick a function that describes the decision boundary and fit its parameters directly. No Bayes' rule, no density. That's next week.

From the reading: Bishop §4.2
  • For two classes the posterior is a sigmoid of the log-odds: $p(\mathcal C_1\mid\mathbf x)=\sigma(a)$ with $a=\ln\frac{p(\mathbf x\mid\mathcal C_1)p(\mathcal C_1)}{p(\mathbf x\mid\mathcal C_2)p(\mathcal C_2)}$ and $\sigma(a)=\frac{1}{1+e^{-a}}$. For $K$ classes it's the softmax of $a_k=\ln p(\mathbf x\mid\mathcal C_k)p(\mathcal C_k)$.
  • With Gaussian classes and a shared covariance, $a$ is linear in $\mathbf x$: $p(\mathcal C_1\mid\mathbf x)=\sigma(\mathbf w^T\mathbf x+w_0)$. Logistic regression (Week 2b) fits exactly this form directly, skipping the Gaussians.
  • Naive Bayes (§4.2.3): assume the features are independent given the class, $p(\mathbf x\mid\mathcal C_k)=\prod_i p(x_i\mid\mathcal C_k)$. Only $p$ one-dimensional densities per class: very few parameters (PRTools naivebc).
  • Bishop §2.5 covers the same kernel and nearest-neighbour estimators as slides 37–51 (Figs. 2.24–2.26).

15Handwriting blueprint

Write each step on paper first, then reveal it to check.

1 · From Gaussians to QDA, LDA and nearest mean

  1. Log of the posterior: $\log p(y_i\mid\mathbf x)=-\frac p2\log2\pi-\frac12\log\det\Sigma_i-\frac12(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu_i)^T\Sigma_i^{-1}(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu_i)+\log p(y_i)-\log p(\mathbf x)$.
  2. Drop the terms that are the same for every class ($-\frac p2\log2\pi$ and $-\log p(\mathbf x)$): that leaves $g_i(\mathbf x)$ of slide 20.
  3. Expand: $(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)^T\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu)=\mathbf x^T\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf x-2\boldsymbol\mu^T\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf x+\boldsymbol\mu^T\Sigma^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu$ (the middle terms are equal because $\Sigma^{-1}$ is symmetric).
  4. $f=g_1-g_2=\mathbf x^T\underbrace{\tfrac12(\Sigma_2^{-1}-\Sigma_1^{-1})}_{W}\mathbf x+\underbrace{(\Sigma_1^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu_1-\Sigma_2^{-1}\boldsymbol\mu_2)^T}_{\mathbf w^T}\mathbf x+w_0$: quadratic (slide 21).
  5. LDA, $\Sigma_1=\Sigma_2=\Sigma$: $W=0$ and $\mathbf w=\Sigma^{-1}(\boldsymbol\mu_1-\boldsymbol\mu_2)$. Linear. (Not $\boldsymbol\mu_2-\boldsymbol\mu_1$ as slide 29 has it.)
  6. Nearest mean, $\Sigma=\sigma^2I$: multiply $f$ by $\sigma^2$ to get $\mathbf w=\boldsymbol\mu_1-\boldsymbol\mu_2$ and $w_0=\frac12\|\boldsymbol\mu_2\|^2-\frac12\|\boldsymbol\mu_1\|^2+\sigma^2\log\frac{p(y_1)}{p(y_2)}$. With equal priors $f>0$ exactly when $\|\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu_1\|<\|\mathbf x-\boldsymbol\mu_2\|$.

2 · A covariance matrix by hand (slides 24–27)

  1. Mean of the class: average the points, e.g. $(2,0),(3,0)\to(2.5,0)$.
  2. Subtract the mean from every point: $(-0.5,0)$ and $(0.5,0)$.
  3. Outer product of each centred point with itself: a $p\times p$ matrix, here $\begin{bmatrix}0.25&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix}$ twice.
  4. Average them (slides divide by $n$): $\hat\Sigma=\begin{bmatrix}0.25&0\\0&0\end{bmatrix}$.
  5. Check invertibility: $\det\hat\Sigma=0.25\cdot0-0\cdot0=0$, so there is no inverse and QDA is undefined. Pool the classes (average the $\hat\Sigma_k$) and check the determinant again.

3 · Why k-NN classification is a majority vote (slides 47–49)

  1. Grow a ball around $\mathbf x$ until it contains $k$ training objects; call its volume $V_k$. Of those, $k_m$ are from class $m$.
  2. Class density: $\hat p(\mathbf x\mid y_m)=\frac{k_m}{n_mV_k}$ ($k_m$ of the $n_m$ class-$m$ objects in volume $V_k$).
  3. Prior: $\hat p(y_m)=\frac{n_m}{n}$.
  4. Multiply: $\hat p(\mathbf x\mid y_m)\hat p(y_m)=\frac{k_m}{nV_k}$. The factor $\frac1{nV_k}$ is the same for every class.
  5. So pick the class with the largest $k_m$, and $\hat p(y_m\mid\mathbf x)=\frac{k_m}{k}$.

4 · Why the training likelihood prefers h → 0 (Exercise 4.1)

  1. Training log-likelihood: $LL=\sum_{i}\log\hat p(\mathbf x_i)$ with $\hat p(\mathbf x_i)=\frac1n\sum_j\mathcal N(\mathbf x_i\mid\mathbf x_j,h^2)$.
  2. The sum includes $j=i$: a kernel evaluated at its own centre, $\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,h}$ in 1-D.
  3. So $\hat p(\mathbf x_i)\ge\frac1n\frac1{\sqrt{2\pi}\,h}\to\infty$ as $h\to0$, and $LL\to\infty$.
  4. A test point $\mathbf z$ is not a kernel centre: for tiny $h$ every kernel is ~0 at $\mathbf z$, so $\log\hat p(\mathbf z)\to-\infty$.
  5. Choose $h$ by the test (or leave-one-out) likelihood, which has a maximum in between.

16Exam traps

Watch out
  • "The covariance matrix controls the location of the Gaussian." No: the mean does. $\Sigma$ controls the spread and orientation (24/25 exam).
  • "With enough data QDA is at least as good as LDA, even if the true boundary is linear." FALSE (24/25 exam). QDA estimates many more numbers, and its extra flexibility can only hurt when LDA's simpler assumption is already right.
  • "QDA needs fewer objects than LDA." The opposite: each class needs $n_k\ge p+1$ for $\hat\Sigma_k$ to be invertible; LDA pools all classes into one matrix.
  • Sign of $\mathbf w$ in LDA. $\mathbf w=\Sigma^{-1}(\boldsymbol\mu_1-\boldsymbol\mu_2)$ if $f>0$ means class 1. Slide 29 has it the other way round.
  • "Parzen and k-NN are insensitive to feature scaling." False. They use Euclidean distances: scale your features. LDA/QDA are the ones that don't care (with enough data).
  • "A small Parzen width $h$ increases the bias." False: small $h$ means low bias and high variance (spiky, overfitted).
  • Choosing $h$ on the training likelihood always gives $h\to0$. Use a test set or leave-one-out.
  • k-NN training error at $k=1$ is 0: each object is its own nearest neighbour. That says nothing about the test error.
  • $k=n$ or $h\to\infty$: always predict the largest class, so the error equals the smaller prior (two classes).
  • Missing class. A density-based classifier can give a class no region at all (slide 8).
  • "More features can't hurt." With a fixed training set they can: the curse of dimensionality.
  • Naive Bayes assumes independence given the class, not independence of the features overall.

17Self-check

Answer first, then open. past exam marks questions from the 24/25 exam reviews.

1. past exam The covariance matrix in a 2-D Gaussian distribution controls: (a) the location (b) the spread and orientation (c) the height only (d) the number of modes
(b). The mean sets the location. The height follows from $\det\Sigma$, but "height only" misses the orientation. A single Gaussian always has one mode.
2. past exam True or false: given enough training data, QDA will find a classifier that is at least as good as LDA, even if the true decision boundary is linear.
False (per the 24/25 answer key). QDA has to estimate a covariance per class: many more numbers, so a noisier, curved boundary. When the truth is linear, LDA's assumption is exactly right and QDA's extra freedom can only add error. Only in the limit of infinite data with truly Gaussian classes would the two coincide; "enough data" isn't that limit. Try it in the widget of Part 8 with "equal Σ".
3. past exam True or false: for LDA with features $x_1,\dots,x_D$ and a scaling factor $s_i$ per feature, you cannot improve LDA's performance by optimising the $s_i$. Assume sufficient data.
True. Scaling features maps $\boldsymbol\mu\to S\boldsymbol\mu$ and $\Sigma\to S\Sigma S$; the $\Sigma^{-1}$ in LDA undoes it, so the boundary (in the original space) is the same. Contrast: nearest mean, k-NN and Parzen do change with scaling.
4. past exam True or false: even if the Bayes classifier is non-linear, the nearest-mean classifier may still outperform the k-nearest-neighbour classifier.
True. With a small training set k-NN's flexible boundary is very noisy (high variance), while nearest mean's straight line is stable. Being closer to the Bayes shape doesn't help if you can't estimate it well.
5. past exam Which statement about k-NN is correct? "When we set $k=n$, the accuracy equals the class prior of the largest class in the training set."
Correct. All $n$ training objects vote for every test object, so the majority class always wins: accuracy = largest prior (error = $\min(p(y_1),p(y_2))$ for two classes, slide 51).
6. past exam Which statement best describes the Parzen classifier? (a) As $h\to\infty$ the accuracy equals the prior of the largest class. (b) We need the class priors to do Parzen classification. (c) A very small $h$ increases the bias. (d) Parzen classifiers are insensitive to the relative scales of the features.
(a). Infinitely wide kernels make every class density flat, so only the priors decide. (b) You only need priors for the Bayes-optimal version; you can classify with the densities alone. (c) Small $h$ decreases bias (and increases variance). (d) It uses distances, so it is sensitive to scaling.
7. past exam You have 50 apples and oranges (no more can be bought) and a histogram-based classifier with 90% hold-out accuracy. An expert suggests adding a few hundred more sensors (a few hundred extra features). Good idea?
No. With 50 objects, a histogram in hundreds of dimensions is almost entirely empty cells: the curse of dimensionality. The extra features make the data relatively less informative, and the accuracy will drop.

8. exam style LDA and QDA on four objects

Two classes, two objects each (slides 24–27, the format of 24/25 exam question 21). (a) Where is the LDA decision boundary? (b) What does QDA give?

Show worked solution

(a) LDA pools the covariances: $\hat\Sigma=\tfrac12\big(\mathrm{diag}(0,1)+\mathrm{diag}(0.25,0)\big)=\mathrm{diag}(0.125,0.5)$, which is invertible. The means differ only in $x_1$ and $\hat\Sigma$ is diagonal, so $\mathbf w$ points along $x_1$: a vertical line. With equal priors it goes through the midpoint of the means, $\tfrac12\big((-1,0)+(2.5,0)\big)=(0.75,0)$, so the boundary is $\mathbf{x_1=0.75}$ (the calculation is in Part 6).

(b) QDA needs $\hat\Sigma_{-1}^{-1}$ and $\hat\Sigma_{+1}^{-1}$, but both class covariances are singular ($\det=0$): the class $-1$ points have no spread in $x_1$, the class $+1$ points none in $x_2$. QDA is not defined.

The 24/25 answer had the same pattern: "LDA: a vertical line between the two class means; QDA: the covariance matrices have no inverse, so QDA is not defined."

9. Two classes, $p=10$ features. How many numbers do QDA, LDA and nearest mean estimate (ignore priors)?
QDA: $2(10+55)=\mathbf{130}$. LDA: $2\cdot10+55=\mathbf{75}$. Nearest mean: $2\cdot10=\mathbf{20}$ (+1 for $\sigma^2$). A symmetric $10\times10$ matrix has $\frac{10\cdot11}2=55$ free numbers.
10. 3 training objects per class, 5 features. Which of QDA, LDA and nearest mean can you train?
Only nearest mean. Each class covariance has rank ≤ 2 < 5 (singular). The pooled covariance combines 2 + 2 = 4 directions: rank ≤ 4 < 5, still singular. Means need just one object per class.
11. k-NN with $k=5$: the neighbours of $\mathbf x$ are 3 of class A and 2 of class B. What are $\hat p(A\mid\mathbf x)$ and the decision?
$\hat p(A\mid\mathbf x)=3/5=0.6$, so assign A.
12. A histogram classifier uses 4 features with 10 bins each and has 200 training objects. At most what fraction of the cells contains any data?
$10^4=10\,000$ cells and at most 200 non-empty, so at most 2%. The other 98% get density 0.

Exercise 3.1 · Classification with normal densities

Two 2-D normal distributions with different means but equal covariance matrices. (a) What shape does the optimal boundary have? Generate 10 points per class. (b) How does the boundary of the trained qdc look? (c) Scatter the data and plot the ldc and qdc boundaries; explain the difference. (d) What happens with more points per class, and in the limit?

You need: QDA vs LDA (§4, §6), and why estimated covariances differ even when the true ones are equal (§8).

Hint

Equal true covariances make LDA's assumption exactly right. Now think about the two estimated covariances from 10 points each: are they equal?

Show worked solution

(a) A straight line (a hyperplane in general): with equal covariances the quadratic terms cancel (blueprint 1).

(b) Curved (a conic). qdc estimates a separate $\hat\Sigma_k$ per class, and from 10 points the two estimates differ by chance, so $W=\frac12(\hat\Sigma_2^{-1}-\hat\Sigma_1^{-1})\ne0$.

(c)

import numpy as np, matplotlib.pyplot as plt, prtools as pr
rng = np.random.default_rng(3)
mu1, mu2, S = [0, 0], [2, 1], [[1, .5], [.5, 1]]      # equal covariances
X = np.r_[rng.multivariate_normal(mu1, S, 10), rng.multivariate_normal(mu2, S, 10)]
y = np.r_[np.ones(10), 2 * np.ones(10)].reshape(-1, 1)
a = pr.prdataset(X, y)
w1 = pr.ldc(); w1.train(a)
w2 = pr.qdc(); w2.train(a)
pr.scatterd(a); pr.plotc([w1, w2])                    # a legend appears with two boundaries

On 10 000 test objects: ldc 15.9%, qdc 16.2% (10 per class); both 15.7% (1000 per class). The Bayes error of this problem is 15.9% (Mahalanobis distance between the means: 2).

(d) With more points the covariance estimates converge to the same true $\Sigma$, so $W\to0$ and the qdc boundary straightens towards the ldc boundary. In the limit both equal the optimal (Bayes) linear boundary, because both models contain the truth. For small samples ldc is better: same truth, fewer numbers to estimate.

Exercise 4.1 · Parzen log-likelihood on the training set

Fit parzenm with $h\in\{0.01,\dots,5\}$ on 40 points from gendats([20,20],1,8) (labels removed) and compute the log-likelihood of the same points. What is the "optimal" $h$? Is that also the best density estimate?

You need: §10 and blueprint 4.

Hint

Look at what each point contributes to its own density estimate when $h$ is tiny.

Show worked solution

The training log-likelihood falls monotonically with $h$: 152.4 at $h=0.01$, 63.2 at $h=1$, 27.8 at $h=5$. So the "optimal" $h$ by this criterion is the smallest one tried, 0.01.

That is not a good density estimate: it is 40 narrow spikes, one per training point, and almost zero everywhere else. Each point's own kernel gives it density $\approx\frac1n\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}h}$, which explodes as $h\to0$ (blueprint 4). The training likelihood rewards memorising, exactly like the apparent error in Week 1a.

Exercise 4.2 · Training versus test log-likelihood

Split the data 50/50 with gendat(a, 0.5), fit on the training half, and plot the log-likelihood of both halves against $h$. What is a good choice for $h$?

You need: §10; play with the density lab first.

Hint

Which of the two curves can tell you how the estimate does on new data? Where is its maximum?

Show worked solution

A good choice is $h\approx0.5$ (anything in about 0.5–1 is close). For smaller $h$ the test points fall between the spikes, so $\hat p\approx0$ and $\log\hat p\to-\infty$: that's the RuntimeWarning: divide by zero encountered in log your notebook printed. It is not a bug: the density is numerically 0 at some test points. For larger $h$ the two bumps get smeared together and the likelihood drops slowly.

The rule of thumb on slide 45 ($p=1$, $n=20$, within-bump $\sigma\approx1$) gives $h\approx0.58$, in the same place.
