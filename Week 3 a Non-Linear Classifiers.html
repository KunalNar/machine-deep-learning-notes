Non-Linear Classifiers

Straight lines are too rigid for data like the banana set. This lecture builds curved boundaries out of simple pieces: feed a linear classifier non-linear features, cut the space into boxes with a decision tree, let many randomised trees vote (bagging, random forests), or let weak classifiers fix each other's mistakes (AdaBoost). Training the combination of classifiers itself leads straight to neural networks.

0Test yourself first

Answer out loud before you read on. Each answer points to the part that explains it.

1. A decision tree trained twice on the same data gives exactly the same tree. Why does that make plain averaging useless, and what does a random forest do about it?
Averaging identical trees gives the same tree again: there is nothing to average out. A random forest adds randomness: every tree gets its own bootstrap sample of the objects, and every node only looks at a random subset of the features. The trees differ, and their mistakes partly cancel in the vote. → Part 6
2. In AdaBoost, what happens to the weight of an object the current weak classifier gets wrong? And how much say does a weak classifier with weighted error 0.5 get?
Its weight goes up (×$e^{\alpha_m}$, then renormalise), so the next weak classifier focuses on it. With $\varepsilon_m=0.5$: $\alpha_m=\tfrac12\ln\frac{0.5}{0.5}=0$, so no say at all; it's no better than a coin flip. → Part 7
3. Is a random forest a generative or a discriminative classifier?
Discriminative: it never estimates the class densities $p(\mathbf x\mid y)$. The trees directly map $\mathbf x$ to a class (or a vote fraction). (A past exam question.) → Part 6

One sentence to remember

Most non-linear classifiers here are built by combining simple ones: thresholds on single features (stumps and trees), many trees that vote (forests), weak classifiers that are added one by one with a weight (boosting), or base classifiers whose outputs are merged by a trained combiner (neural networks). 2Why non-linear, and the price slides 1–11

Week 2b's classifiers draw straight lines. The banana set on slide 1 needs a curved boundary. More flexible models can bend, but they have more parameters, so they need more data. Slides 2–8 recap why that hurts.

  • Curse of dimensionality (slides 3–4): a histogram with 10 bins per feature needs $10^p$ cells: 10 in 1-D, 100 in 2-D, $10^{50}$ in 50-D. The data you need can grow exponentially with the number of features. More in Week 1b §13.
  • Data lives in the tails (slide 5): see the numbers below.
  • Feature curve (slides 6–8): the test error first drops, then rises as complexity grows (bias falls, variance rises), and the best complexity moves right with more data. See Week 2b §9.
Tiny numbers (slide 5)

The fraction of a ball's volume inside 90% of its radius is $\frac{\text{Vol}(0.9r)}{\text{Vol}(r)}=0.9^p$: 0.9 in 1-D, $0.9^{10}=0.35$ in 10-D, $0.9^{50}=0.005$ in 50-D. In 50 dimensions 99.5% of the volume sits in the thin outer shell: "all the data is in the rim".

Slide 9 recaps the two routes: generative models estimate $\hat p(\mathbf x\mid y)$ (fine with enough data); discriminative models choose $\hat p(y\mid\mathbf x)=f(\mathbf x;\mathbf w)$ directly, which may need less data, but how do you choose $f$? Last week $f$ was linear. Slide 11 lists the non-linear options: non-linear features, support vector classifiers (Week 5), decision trees, random forests, boosting and AdaBoost, combining classifiers, and neural networks (Week 3b).

3Non-linear features slides 12–14

Intuition

Keep the linear classifier, but give it new features made from the old ones: $x_1^2$, $x_1x_2$, $\sin x_2$… The model is still a weighted sum, so it is linear in the weights: every training method from Week 2b works unchanged. But in the original features the boundary is now curved.

Formula (slide 12)
$$\hat y=w_2x_2+w_1x_1+w_0\ \longrightarrow\ \hat y=w_5x_2^2+w_4x_1^2+w_3x_1x_2+w_2x_2+w_1x_1+w_0$$

The quadratic version has a conic boundary (ellipse, parabola or hyperbola), like QDA's in Week 1b. Other functions work too: $\hat y=w_4\sin(x_2)+w_3\cos(x_1)+w_2x_2+w_1x_1+w_0$.

Slide 12 asks: advantages and disadvantages?
✓Any linear training method works: least squares in closed form, the perceptron, logistic regression.
✓Very flexible: with the right features almost any boundary is possible.
✗You have to choose the features yourself.
✗The number of features explodes: all monomials up to degree $d$ in $p$ features are $\binom{p+d}{d}$ terms.
✗More weights need more data, so overfitting and wild extrapolation follow. In Assignment 4 Ex 2.1, degree 20 gave a test MSE of about $10^5$.
Tiny numbers

$\binom{p+d}{d}$ features (constant included): $p=2,d=2$: $\binom42=6$, exactly the six weights $w_0,\dots,w_5$ above. $p=10,d=3$: $\binom{13}{3}=286$. $p=100,d=3$: $\binom{103}{3}=176\,851$.

Preview: the support vector classifier and the kernel trick (slides 13–14)

The SVC puts the boundary in the middle of the widest band between the classes: the margin between $\mathbf w^T\mathbf x+w_0=+1$ and $-1$. The kernel trick makes it non-linear: it computes inner products of (huge, even infinite) feature vectors without ever building them. That is Week 5: see Week 5a §5 (margin) and §9 (kernels).

4Growing a decision tree slides 15–18 Bishop §14.4

Intuition

A tree plays "twenty questions" with the object. Every internal node asks one yes/no question about one feature; the answer sends the object left or right; a leaf says the class. Each question cuts the space with a line parallel to an axis, so the leaves are boxes.

How a tree is grown (greedy, top-down)

  1. Start with all training objects in the root.
  2. For every feature and every threshold (midpoints between neighbouring sorted values), compute how much the split lowers the impurity. This exhaustive search is cheap: $p$ features × $(N-1)$ thresholds.
  3. Keep the best split, send the objects left and right, and repeat in both children (slide 18).
  4. Stop when the impurity no longer improves, the node is pure (enough), the maximum depth is reached, or too few objects are left (slide 18). The leaf predicts its majority class.

This is greedy: each split is the best one right now, with no look-ahead. Finding the best tree overall is combinatorially hopeless (Bishop §14.4).

Formula: impurity (slide 16)

With $p_k$ the fraction of class $k$ among the objects in the node ("estimated by counting"):

$$Q_{\text{ent}}=-\sum_{k=1}^{C}p_k\log p_k,\qquad Q_{\text{Gini}}=\sum_{k=1}^{C}p_k(1-p_k)=1-\sum_k p_k^2,\qquad Q_{\text{err}}=1-\max_k p_k.$$

A split sends $N_L$ of the node's $N$ objects left and $N_R$ right. Pick the split with the largest impurity drop (for entropy this is the information gain):

$$\Delta Q=Q(\text{node})-\frac{N_L}{N}\,Q(\text{left})-\frac{N_R}{N}\,Q(\text{right}).$$

Slide 16 (like Bishop eq. 14.32) writes the entropy without the minus sign and says "split if the impurity can be increased". With the minus sign, both measures are 0 for a pure node, and you split to make the impurity go down.

The decision stump (slide 17)

A stump stops after the root: one split, two leaves. It is extremely weak (about 20% error on the banana set), but it is the only model for which we can really minimise the classification error: try all $p(N-1)$ splits and keep the one with the fewest mistakes. That makes it the favourite weak learner for boosting (Part 7).

Why so unstable?

The greedy search picks the best split by a small margin; one object less and another split wins. Every split below depends on that choice, so the change cascades down the tree. Splits are also hard and axis-parallel: a diagonal boundary needs a staircase of many small boxes (Bishop §14.4). Try the "diagonal boundary" data below.

How to stabilise a tree (slide 22)

  • Simplify: stop early, or grow a large tree and prune it back. Bishop's pruning criterion is $C(T)=\sum_{\tau}Q_\tau(T)+\lambda|T|$: impurity plus a penalty per leaf, with $\lambda$ chosen by cross-validation.
  • Averaging alone does not work: the same data gives exactly the same tree.
  • Introduce randomness: subsample the training data (bootstrap) and subsample the features (maybe even per node). The result is a random forest.

6Bagging and random forests slides 22–25 Bishop §14.2

Intuition

Ask many slightly different experts and take the vote. Each tree overfits its own sample in its own way. Where they disagree, the mistakes partly cancel; where the signal is real, they agree. Averaging attacks the variance, which is exactly the problem of a deep tree.

Formula (slides 23–24)

Random forest: repeat $M$ times: (1) draw a bootstrap sample of $N$ objects, (2) grow a tree $\hat y_m(\mathbf x)$, choosing each split from a random subset of the features. (3) Average:

$$\hat y_{\text{bag}}(\mathbf x)=\frac1M\sum_{m=1}^{M}\hat y_m(\mathbf x).$$

The same recipe with any base classifier is bagging (Breiman 1996). "Under some (quite strong) assumptions" (Bishop eq. 14.12–14.14: the errors $\epsilon_m(\mathbf x)$ of the members have zero mean and are uncorrelated):

$$E_{\text{bag}}=\frac1M\,E_{\text{AV}}.$$

In practice the members' errors are highly correlated (they learned from nearly the same data). With equal error variances and correlation $\rho$:

$$E_{\text{bag}}=E_{\text{AV}}\Big(\rho+\frac{1-\rho}{M}\Big)\ \xrightarrow{M\to\infty}\ \rho\,E_{\text{AV}},$$

and Bishop shows that always $E_{\text{bag}}\le E_{\text{AV}}$. That is why random forests decorrelate their trees with random feature subsets.

Tiny numbers
  • One draw misses object $i$ with probability $1-\frac1N$, so $N$ draws miss it with probability $(1-\frac1N)^N$: $(7/8)^8=0.34$ for $N=8$, and $\to e^{-1}=0.368$ for large $N$. A bootstrap sample holds about 63.2% of the distinct objects.
  • $M=10$, $E_{\text{AV}}=0.2$: uncorrelated gives $0.02$; with $\rho=0.5$ it is $0.2\,(0.5+0.05)=0.11$.
  • Over 20 random banana training sets (100 objects, noise 1.5): one full tree 12.1% test error, bagging 100 trees 10.2%, random forest 100 trees 9.4%.

7Boosting and AdaBoost slides 26–31 Bishop §14.3

Intuition

Bagging trains independent voters. Boosting trains a team in sequence: each new weak classifier concentrates on the objects the team so far gets wrong, and better members get a bigger say. Many weak learners, each only a bit better than guessing, add up to a strong classifier.

Formula: the AdaBoost algorithm (slides 26–31)

Labels $y_i\in\{-1,+1\}$, weak classifiers $\hat y_m(\mathbf x)\in\{-1,+1\}$. The final classifier is a weighted vote (slide 26):

$$F_K(\mathbf x)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\alpha_k\,\hat y_k(\mathbf x),\qquad \text{class}=\operatorname{sign}F_K(\mathbf x).$$

Optimising all members and weights at once is very hard, so do it incrementally (slide 28):

  1. Give every object the same weight, $w_i=1/N$.
  2. Train a weak classifier $\hat y_m$ on the weighted objects (e.g. a stump that minimises the weighted error).
  3. Its weighted error: $\varepsilon_m=\sum_{i:\,\hat y_m(\mathbf x_i)\neq y_i}w_i$ (weights summing to 1).
  4. Its vote: $\alpha_m=\tfrac12\ln\dfrac{1-\varepsilon_m}{\varepsilon_m}$.
  5. Reweight: $w_i\leftarrow w_i\,e^{-\alpha_m y_i\hat y_m(\mathbf x_i)}$, so misclassified objects get ×$e^{+\alpha_m}$ and correct ones ×$e^{-\alpha_m}$. Then renormalise.
  6. Go to 2 for as long as needed.

Slide 28, step 5 says "multiply weights of erroneously classified objects with $\exp(-\alpha_m)$". That would lower their weight; it must be $\exp(+\alpha_m)$. Slide 29's formula $w_i=\exp\big(-y_i\sum_k\alpha_kf_k(\mathbf x_i)\big)$ has it right. Bishop (eq. 14.17–14.18) uses $\alpha_m=\ln\frac{1-\varepsilon_m}{\varepsilon_m}$, twice as large: same sign, same classifier.

Tiny numbers: one round by hand

Six objects at $x=1,\dots,6$ with labels $+,+,-,-,+,-$; all weights $\tfrac16$. The best stump, "$x<2.5\Rightarrow+$", misses only $x=5$: $\varepsilon_1=\tfrac16$, $\alpha_1=\tfrac12\ln5=0.805$. Reweight: $x=5$ gets $\tfrac16e^{0.805}=\tfrac16\sqrt5$, the others $\tfrac16/\sqrt5$; after normalising, $x=5$ has weight $\tfrac12$ and the other five $\tfrac1{10}$ each.

Notice: after reweighting, the stump you just used has weighted error exactly $\tfrac12$ (its mistakes now carry half the weight). The next round can't pick it again; it has to fix $x=5$.

The exam question in one line

Training labels with 5% flipped, clean test set: bagging with uniform bootstrap samples is the safer choice. Sampling objects in proportion to their current loss is boosting in disguise, and the objects with the highest loss are mostly the flipped ones.

8Combining classifiers slides 32–40

Intuition

Different classifiers make different mistakes, so merge their outputs. The catch is in the word "different": combining only helps when the base classifiers disagree in useful ways, and a fixed rule can't know which classifier to trust where.

  • Fixed combiners (slide 32): average the posteriors (the most common rule), or take their product, maximum, or a majority vote.
  • Identical base classifiers (slides 33–34: LDA, LDA, LDA on the same data): the combination is just LDA. Nothing gained.
  • Similar base classifiers (slide 35: LDA, logistic, perceptron): all linear, so still very similar.
  • Diverse base classifiers (slides 37–38: LDA, QDC, Parzen): now they differ, but a fixed rule is often not better than the best one.
  • Trained combiner (slide 39): learn how much to trust each base classifier, for example a classifier trained on their outputs (often called stacking). Less restrictive base outputs, such as raw scores instead of hard labels, may help. Train the combiner on data the base classifiers did not see, or it will trust the most overfitted member.
  • Training everything at once (slide 40): with perceptrons as base classifiers, how do you train the base classifiers and the combiner together? "Not straightforward!" The answer is error backpropagation: Week 3b.
Tiny numbers: rules can disagree

Three classifiers give $\hat p(\omega_1\mid\mathbf x)=0.9,\ 0.4,\ 0.45$. Mean rule: $\frac{0.9+0.4+0.45}{3}=0.583>0.5$, so $\omega_1$. Majority vote: one vote for $\omega_1$, two for $\omega_2$, so $\omega_2$. The mean listens to how confident each classifier is; the vote does not.

Independence pays: a majority vote of independent classifiers that are each right 70% of the time is right 78.4% of the time with 3 members, 83.7% with 5 and 92.2% with 11. Three identical copies stay at 70% (figure in Part 6).

10Handwriting blueprint

Write each step on paper first, then reveal it.

1 · The impurity drop of a split

  1. Count the classes in the node and in both children, e.g. node (5 A, 5 B) → left (5 A, 2 B), right (0 A, 3 B).
  2. Turn counts into fractions $p_k$ and compute $Q$ for each: node $Q_{\text{Gini}}=1-0.5^2-0.5^2=0.5$; left $1-(5/7)^2-(2/7)^2=20/49=0.408$; right $0$.
  3. Weight the children by their share of the objects: $\frac7{10}\cdot0.408+\frac3{10}\cdot0=0.286$.
  4. Drop: $\Delta Q=0.5-0.286=0.214$.
  5. Repeat for every candidate split and keep the largest $\Delta Q$.

2 · One AdaBoost round

  1. Start from the current weights (round 1: all $1/N$).
  2. Find the weak classifier with the smallest weighted error; $\varepsilon_m$ = the summed weight of the objects it gets wrong.
  3. $\alpha_m=\tfrac12\ln\frac{1-\varepsilon_m}{\varepsilon_m}$.
  4. Multiply the weights of the wrong objects by $e^{\alpha_m}=\sqrt{\frac{1-\varepsilon_m}{\varepsilon_m}}$ and of the correct objects by $e^{-\alpha_m}$.
  5. Renormalise to sum 1. Check: the wrong objects now carry exactly half of the weight.
  6. Add $\alpha_m\hat y_m(\mathbf x)$ to $F(\mathbf x)$; the class is $\operatorname{sign}F(\mathbf x)$.

3 · Where $\alpha=\tfrac12\ln\frac{1-\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}$ comes from beyond the slides

  1. AdaBoost greedily minimises the exponential loss $\sum_iw_i\,e^{-\alpha y_i\hat y(\mathbf x_i)}$ over the new vote $\alpha$ (Bishop §14.3.1).
  2. Correct objects contribute $e^{-\alpha}$, wrong ones $e^{+\alpha}$. With weights summing to 1: $E(\alpha)=(1-\varepsilon)e^{-\alpha}+\varepsilon e^{\alpha}$.
  3. Derivative: $E'(\alpha)=-(1-\varepsilon)e^{-\alpha}+\varepsilon e^{\alpha}$.
  4. Set to 0: $e^{2\alpha}=\frac{1-\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}$, so $\alpha=\tfrac12\ln\frac{1-\varepsilon}{\varepsilon}$.
  5. The new weights $w_i\,e^{-\alpha y_i\hat y(\mathbf x_i)}$ are exactly the terms of this loss: objects that contribute a lot of loss get a lot of weight.

4 · Committee error with correlated members

  1. Member errors $\epsilon_m(\mathbf x)$ with $\mathbb E[\epsilon_m]=0$, $\mathbb E[\epsilon_m^2]=\sigma^2=E_{\text{AV}}$, $\mathbb E[\epsilon_m\epsilon_l]=\rho\sigma^2$ for $m\neq l$.
  2. Committee error: $E_{\text{COM}}=\mathbb E\Big[\big(\tfrac1M\sum_m\epsilon_m\big)^2\Big]=\frac1{M^2}\sum_m\sum_l\mathbb E[\epsilon_m\epsilon_l]$.
  3. $M$ diagonal terms give $M\sigma^2$; $M(M-1)$ off-diagonal terms give $M(M-1)\rho\sigma^2$.
  4. $E_{\text{COM}}=\frac{\sigma^2}{M}+\frac{M-1}{M}\rho\sigma^2=E_{\text{AV}}\big(\rho+\frac{1-\rho}{M}\big)$.
  5. $\rho=0$ gives slide 24's $E_{\text{AV}}/M$; $\rho=1$ (identical members) gives $E_{\text{AV}}$: no gain.

5 · How much of the data a bootstrap sample sees

  1. One draw picks object $i$ with probability $\frac1N$, so it misses it with probability $1-\frac1N$.
  2. The $N$ draws are independent: missed every time with probability $\big(1-\frac1N\big)^N$.
  3. Limit: $\big(1-\frac1N\big)^N\to e^{-1}\approx0.368$.
  4. So a sample contains about $1-0.368=63.2\%$ of the distinct objects; the other 36.8% are "out of bag".

11Exam traps

Watch out
  • "Discriminative" means "no class densities", not the societal meaning. A random forest is discriminative because it never estimates $p(\mathbf x\mid y)$ (24/25 exam review).
  • AdaBoost's principle: combine many weak classifiers into one strong classifier (24/25 exam).
  • Boosting is sensitive to label noise; bagging with uniform bootstrap samples is robust to it (exam review).
  • Slide 28's step 5 has the wrong sign: misclassified objects are multiplied by $e^{+\alpha_m}$, not $e^{-\alpha_m}$.
  • Slide 16's entropy misses its minus sign, so "increase the impurity" is backwards: impurity is 0 for a pure node and you split to decrease it.
  • Averaging identical classifiers changes nothing. Diversity comes from bootstrap samples, random feature subsets, reweighting, or different model types.
  • Bagging reduces variance, not bias. Bagging stumps or LDA gains little; bagging deep trees gains a lot.
  • $E_{\text{bag}}=E_{\text{AV}}/M$ only holds for uncorrelated, zero-mean errors. Real members are correlated; you only get "never worse than average": $E_{\text{bag}}\le E_{\text{AV}}$.
  • Tree boundaries are axis-parallel, and so are AdaBoost-with-stumps boundaries: a staircase, never a smooth curve.
  • Boosted stumps are additive, $F(\mathbf x)=f_1(x_1)+f_2(x_2)$: they can't solve XOR. Use deeper trees as weak learners for interactions.
  • Trees don't care about feature scaling (thresholds move along); k-NN, Parzen, SVMs and the perceptron do.
  • A fully grown tree has zero training error (unless identical inputs carry different labels). That says nothing about the test error.
  • $\varepsilon_m=0.5$ gives $\alpha_m=0$; $\varepsilon_m>0.5$ gives a negative vote (the classifier is used upside down).
  • Non-linear features keep the classifier linear in the weights. All of Week 2b applies; only the feature space changed.
  • A fixed combiner is often no better than the best base classifier (slide 38); a trained combiner needs its own, unseen training data.

Creating a strong classifier by combining multiple weak classifiers. They are trained one after the other on reweighted data, and each gets a vote $\alpha_m$ based on its weighted error.
2. past exam Is the random forest classifier a generative or a discriminative classifier? Why?
Discriminative, because it does not estimate the densities of each class. The trees map $\mathbf x$ straight to a class. The tempting wrong option ("discriminative, since it can discriminate based on sensitive attributes") uses the societal meaning of the word.
3. past exam Ensembles of decision trees; about 5% of the training labels are randomly flipped, the test set is clean. Which is most likely to improve test performance: (a) bagging many trees with uniform bootstrap samples, or (b) bagging many trees and sampling points with a probability proportional to their current loss?
(a). Option (b) is boosting in disguise: the points with the highest loss are mostly the flipped ones, so later trees are trained mainly on noise. Uniform bootstrap sampling treats noisy points like any other, and averaging many deep (low-bias, high-variance) trees cancels the variance the noise causes.
4. Use the tree in Part 4. Classify $(1,1)$, $(3,1)$, $(3,4)$, $(5,4)$ and $(5,6)$.
$(1,1)$: $x_1<4$, $x_2<3$, $x_1<2$ → B. $(3,1)$: yes, yes, no → A. $(3,4)$: $x_1<4$, $x_2\ge3$ → B. $(5,4)$: $x_1\ge4$, $x_2<5$ → A. $(5,6)$: $x_1\ge4$, $x_2\ge5$ → B.
5. A node holds 6 objects of class A and 2 of class B. Compute its Gini impurity, entropy (bits) and misclassification rate.
$p=(0.75,0.25)$. Gini: $1-0.75^2-0.25^2=0.375$. Entropy: $-0.75\log_20.75-0.25\log_20.25=0.311+0.5=0.811$ bits. Error: $1-0.75=0.25$.
6. A stump has weighted error $\varepsilon=0.25$. Give $\alpha$, the factors for the wrong and the correct objects, and the total weight of the wrong objects after normalising.
$\alpha=\tfrac12\ln3=0.549$. Wrong: ×$e^{\alpha}=\sqrt3=1.732$; correct: ×$e^{-\alpha}=0.577$. Before normalising: $0.25\cdot1.732=0.433$ and $0.75\cdot0.577=0.433$, equal. After normalising the wrong objects hold exactly $\tfrac12$.
7. A training set has 1000 objects. How many distinct objects does a bootstrap sample contain on average?
$1000\,\big(1-(1-\tfrac1{1000})^{1000}\big)\approx1000\,(1-0.368)\approx632$.
8. True or false? (a) Bagging decision stumps improves them a lot. (b) Rescaling a feature changes a decision tree's predictions. (c) Random forests choose from a random subset of features at each node to make the trees less correlated. (d) AdaBoost with stumps can solve the XOR problem.
(a) False: stumps are stable and high-bias; bagging only cancels variance. (b) False: the threshold scales along. (c) True. (d) False: a sum of stumps is $f_1(x_1)+f_2(x_2)$, and no such sum is positive on $(+,+)$ and $(-,-)$ but negative on $(+,-)$ and $(-,+)$: the two pairs have the same total $f_1(+)+f_1(-)+f_2(+)+f_2(-)$.
9. Three classifiers give $\hat p(\omega_1\mid\mathbf x)=0.9,\ 0.4,\ 0.45$. What do the mean rule and the majority vote decide?
Mean $0.583$ → $\omega_1$. Vote 1 : 2 → $\omega_2$.
10. You expand 10 features into all monomials up to degree 3 (constant included). How many weights does a linear classifier on these features have?
$\binom{10+3}{3}=\binom{13}{3}=286$.

  • ✓ Stump: "severely underfits; a single horizontal or vertical cut." Correct.
  • ✓ Tree: "a mosaic of rectangular boxes, a staircase to approximate diagonal or curved boundaries." Correct, and the reason is that every split tests one feature.
  • ✓ Depth: depth 1 underfits, unlimited depth memorises the training set (100% training accuracy). Correct; that's the bias–variance trade-off with depth as the knob.
  • ✗ AdaBoost: "smooth piecewise linear curves… smoothly approximates complex non-linear curves." It is also additive, $f_1(x_1)+f_2(x_2)$, so it can't model interactions such as XOR.

Polynomial featuresall monomials up to degree $d$ in $p$ features: $\binom{p+d}{d}$ terms; still linear in $\mathbf w$

Bagging$\hat y_{\text{bag}}(\mathbf x)=\frac1M\sum_m\hat y_m(\mathbf x)$; $E_{\text{bag}}=\frac1ME_{\text{AV}}$ if errors uncorrelated; $E_{\text{AV}}(\rho+\frac{1-\rho}{M})$ with correlation $\rho$
