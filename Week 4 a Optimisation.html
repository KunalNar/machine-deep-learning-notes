Machine & Deep Learning · Week 4a

Optimisation

Plain gradient descent uses one learning rate for millions of parameters, follows noisy mini-batch gradients, and crawls through ravines. This lecture fixes it with statistics of past gradients, kept cheaply by an exponentially weighted moving average. Momentum averages their direction, RMSprop their size, and Adam does both. Then normalisation, of the inputs and with batch norm of the hidden features, reshapes the problem itself so that one learning rate fits better.

0Test yourself first

Answer out loud before you read on. Each answer points to the part that explains it.

1. Momentum, RMSprop, Adam: which keeps an average of past gradients, which an average of their squares, which both?
Momentum averages the gradients (their direction); RMSprop averages the squared gradients (their size) and divides the step by its square root; Adam does both and adds bias correction. → Part 5
2. An EWMA with ρ = 0.95 that starts at $S_0=0$ is far too low for the first steps. Why, and what is the fix?
The first value only gets weight $1-\rho=0.05$, and there is nothing before it, so $S_1=0.05\,y_1$. Divide by $1-\rho^t$: $\hat S_t=S_t/(1-\rho^t)$, which makes the weights sum to 1. → Part 4
3. Batch norm normalises with the mean and variance of the mini-batch. At test time you classify one image. Which mean and variance do you use?
The running averages (an EWMA) of the batch means and variances collected during training, plus the learned $\gamma$ and $\beta$. (A past exam question.) → Part 7

One sentence to remember

All three algorithms keep exponentially weighted moving averages of past gradients: of the gradient itself (momentum: "keep going where you were going"), of its square (RMSprop: "take smaller steps where gradients are big"), or of both (Adam). Normalisation makes the loss surface rounder so that any learning rate works better.

2SGD and the learning rate slides 3–8 DL book 5.9, 8.3.1

Formula (slide 4)
$$J(\theta)=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^mL\big(\mathbf x^{(i)},y^{(i)},\theta\big),\qquad\nabla_\theta J(\theta)=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m\nabla_\theta L\big(\mathbf x^{(i)},y^{(i)},\theta\big).$$

Gradient descent: $\theta^*=\theta-\epsilon\,\nabla_\theta J(\theta)$, with the exact gradient over all $m$ examples. Stochastic gradient descent: use a mini-batch of $k$ examples, an unbiased but noisy estimate that is much cheaper:

$$\theta^*=\theta-\epsilon\,\frac1k\sum_{i=1}^{k}\nabla_\theta L\big(\mathbf x^{(i)},y^{(i)},\theta\big).$$
  • Too high (slide 5): the steps overshoot and the path never settles, or diverges.
  • Too low (slide 6): it gets there, but takes very long.
  • Noise (slide 7): SGD keeps bouncing even near a good place. Fix: decay the learning rate over time.
Tiny numbers: the 24/25 exam question

The loss is $L(w)=aw^2+b$.

$L'(w)=2aw$, so $w_{\text{opt}}=0$. One step: $w\leftarrow w-\eta\cdot2aw=(1-2a\eta)\,w$. After $t$ steps $w_t=(1-2a\eta)^tw_0$.

  • Converges if and only if $|1-2a\eta|<1$, i.e. $0<\eta<\frac1a$.
  • Fastest at $\eta_{\text{opt}}=\frac1{2a}$: the factor is 0, so one step lands exactly on the minimum.
  • $\frac1{2a}<\eta<\frac1a$: converges while zig-zagging around 0. Bigger is slower here: with $a=1$, $\eta=0.4$ gives factor 0.2 but $\eta=0.9$ gives factor $-0.8$. So "a larger learning rate always converges faster" is FALSE.
  • Sharper curvature (larger $a$) means a smaller $\eta_{\text{opt}}$.

The 24/25 review calls $\frac1{2a}$ "$\eta_{\max}$". It is the optimal rate; the maximum (the edge of convergence) is $\frac1a$.

3One learning rate is not enough slides 9–10

Intuition

Real loss surfaces are ravines: steep across, almost flat along. One learning rate can't fit both: small enough to be stable across the ravine means far too small along it, so the path zig-zags across while it crawls forward. Slide 9 asks three questions about the zig-zag path, and each answer is an algorithm.

Slide 9's questionAnswerAlgorithm
Would you use the same learning rate for the x- and the y-parameter?No: a smaller one for the steep direction.a learning rate per parameter
Looking at the history, what about the update direction?Along the ravine the past gradients agree: go faster. Across it they flip sign: average them away.momentum
Looking at the history, what about the update size?Where the past gradients were large, take smaller steps.RMSprop
Both?Combine them.Adam

All three need statistics of past gradients, for every one of maybe a billion parameters. Storing the last $k$ gradients would need $k$ copies of the whole network (slide 11). The next part shows how to do it with one extra number per parameter.

4Exponentially weighted moving averages slides 11–17

Intuition

Treat a parameter's gradient as a noisy time series (slide 11). Recent values matter more than old ones, because they were computed at parameters closer to the current ones. An EWMA keeps one running number and updates it with each new value: a little of the new value, most of the old average.

Formula (slides 12, 14, 17)
$$S_t=\rho\,S_{t-1}+(1-\rho)\,y_t,\qquad S_0=0,\quad0<\rho<1.$$

Unrolled, it is a weighted sum with exponentially shrinking weights:

$$S_t=(1-\rho)\,y_t+(1-\rho)\rho\,y_{t-1}+(1-\rho)\rho^2\,y_{t-2}+\dots$$

The weights fade over about $\frac1{1-\rho}$ steps (10 for $\rho=0.9$, 20 for $0.95$, 100 for $0.99$). Memory: for $n$ parameters only $n$ extra numbers (slide 14). Bias correction (slide 17): the weights of the first $t$ values sum to $1-\rho^t$, not 1, so

$$\hat S_t=\frac{S_t}{1-\rho^t}.$$

For large $t$, $\rho^t\approx0$ and the correction disappears.

Tiny numbers (slide 17)

$\rho=0.95$: $S_1=0.05\,y_1$ and $S_2=0.95\cdot0.05\,y_1+0.05\,y_2=0.0475\,y_1+0.05\,y_2$, far too small at the start. The correction divides by $1-0.95^2=0.0975=0.0475+0.05$: exactly the sum of the weights. Another one: $\rho=0.9$, $y=(4,6)$: $S_1=0.4$, $S_2=0.96$; corrected $\hat S_1=4$, $\hat S_2=0.96/0.19=5.05$.

5Momentum, RMSprop and Adam slides 18–22 DL book 8.3.2, 8.5.2, 8.5.3

Formula (slides 19–21)

$\mathbf g=\nabla_\theta$ of the mini-batch in iteration $i$; all operations are per parameter.

Momentum: average the gradients. Default $\rho=0.9$, usually no bias correction.

$$\mathbf v_i=\rho\,\mathbf v_{i-1}+(1-\rho)\,\mathbf g,\qquad\theta^*=\theta-\epsilon\,\mathbf v_i.$$

RMSprop: average the squared gradients, divide by the root. Default $\rho=0.9$, $\delta\approx10^{-6}$ against dividing by 0, usually no bias correction.

$$\mathbf r_i=\rho\,\mathbf r_{i-1}+(1-\rho)\,\mathbf g^2,\qquad\theta^*=\theta-\epsilon\,\frac{\mathbf g}{\sqrt{\mathbf r_i+\delta}}.$$

Adam: both, with bias correction. Defaults $\rho_1=0.9$, $\rho_2=0.999$.

$$\mathbf v_i=\rho_1\mathbf v_{i-1}+(1-\rho_1)\mathbf g,\quad\mathbf r_i=\rho_2\mathbf r_{i-1}+(1-\rho_2)\mathbf g^2,\quad\hat{\mathbf v}_i=\frac{\mathbf v_i}{1-\rho_1^i},\quad\hat{\mathbf r}_i=\frac{\mathbf r_i}{1-\rho_2^i},\quad\theta'=\theta-\epsilon\,\frac{\hat{\mathbf v}_i}{\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf r}_i+\delta}}.$$
Why they work
  • Momentum: across a ravine the gradient flips sign every step, so the average cancels it; along the ravine it keeps the same sign, so the average keeps it. It also smooths mini-batch noise.
  • RMSprop: $\sqrt{\mathbf r}$ is the typical gradient size of each parameter. Dividing by it gives every parameter a step of about $\epsilon$, whether its gradients are big (steep direction: smaller steps) or tiny (flat direction: bigger steps).
  • Adam: the direction of momentum with the per-parameter scaling of RMSprop. Bias correction matters because $\rho_2=0.999$: without it, $\mathbf r$ would stay far too small for the first few hundred steps.

Two versions of momentum

The slides average: $\mathbf v_i=\rho\mathbf v_{i-1}+(1-\rho)\mathbf g$. The DL book and PyTorch's SGD(momentum=0.9) sum: $\mathbf v_i=\rho\mathbf v_{i-1}+\mathbf g$, which is $\frac1{1-\rho}=10$ times larger in the long run. So PyTorch momentum with lr=0.01 moves like the slides' version with $\epsilon=0.1$.

6Normalising the inputs slide 23

Intuition

If one feature is measured in thousands and another in fractions, the loss bowl is a long, thin ellipse: a ravine created by the units alone. Centre every feature at 0 with variance 1 and the bowl becomes round. Then the gradient points at the minimum and one learning rate fits every direction.

Formula (slide 23)
$$\boldsymbol\mu=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m\mathbf x^{(i)},\qquad\boldsymbol\sigma^2=\frac1m\sum_{i=1}^m\big(\mathbf x^{(i)}-\boldsymbol\mu\big)^2,\qquad X\leftarrow\frac{X-\boldsymbol\mu}{\sqrt{\boldsymbol\sigma^2}}\quad\text{(per feature)}.$$

Compute $\boldsymbol\mu$ and $\boldsymbol\sigma$ on the training set and apply the same numbers to validation and test data (Week 4b explains why).

7Batch normalisation slides 24–26 DL book 8.7.1

Intuition

Normalising the inputs helps the first layer. But every hidden layer is the "input" of the next one, and its values shift while the network trains. Batch norm standardises each hidden unit too, using the statistics of the current mini-batch. Forcing mean 0 and variance 1 can be too rigid, so each unit gets a learned scale $\gamma$ and shift $\beta$.

Formula (slide 25)

For one hidden unit, over the $k$ examples of a mini-batch:

$$\mu=\frac1k\sum_{i=1}^kz_i,\qquad\sigma^2=\frac1k\sum_{i=1}^k(z_i-\mu)^2,\qquad\hat z_i=\frac{z_i-\mu}{\sqrt{\delta+\sigma^2}},\qquad\tilde z_i=\gamma\,\hat z_i+\beta.$$

$\delta\approx10^{-8}$ prevents division by 0. Applied to the pre-activation $z=\mathbf x^T\mathbf W_{:,j}+b_j$, before the activation $g$ (slide 24: "there is debate; but before"). The network can undo it all: $\gamma=\sqrt{\delta+\sigma^2}$, $\beta=\mu$ gives back $z$.

Tiny numbers

Mini-batch values of one unit: $z=(1,2,3,6)$. $\mu=3$, $\sigma^2=\frac{4+1+0+9}4=3.5$, $\sigma=1.871$. $\hat z=(-1.07,\,-0.53,\,0,\,1.60)$. With $\gamma=2$, $\beta=1$: $\tilde z=(-1.14,\,-0.07,\,1,\,4.21)$. To undo it, the network would learn $\gamma=1.871$ and $\beta=3$.

At test time (slide 26)

A test example has no mini-batch to compute $\mu$ and $\sigma^2$ from (and its prediction shouldn't depend on which other examples happen to be in the batch). During training, keep an EWMA of the batch means and variances; at test time use those running averages, with the learned $\gamma$ and $\beta$. In PyTorch this is the switch between model.train() and model.eval().

In code: batch norm forward with running averages (basic tensor ops)
def batchnorm(z, gamma, beta, run_mu, run_var, train, rho=0.9, delta=1e-5):
    # z: (k, units); statistics per unit = per column, over the mini-batch
    if train:
        mu, var = z.mean(dim=0), z.var(dim=0, unbiased=False)
        run_mu = rho * run_mu + (1 - rho) * mu        # EWMA for test time
        run_var = rho * run_var + (1 - rho) * var
    else:
        mu, var = run_mu, run_var                     # test time: no batch statistics
    z_hat = (z - mu) / torch.sqrt(var + delta)
    return gamma * z_hat + beta, run_mu, run_var

PyTorch's BatchNorm1d(momentum=0.1) means $1-\rho=0.1$ in this notation.

  • Per unit, BN adds 2 learned parameters ($\gamma,\beta$) and keeps 2 running statistics that are not learned.
  • BN makes training less sensitive to the initial weights and allows larger learning rates. It does not remove the need for a sensible initialisation (a past exam asked exactly that).

9Handwriting blueprint

Write each step on paper first, then reveal it.

1 · Gradient descent on $L(w)=aw^2+b$

  1. $L'(w)=2aw$; setting it to 0 gives $w_{\text{opt}}=0$ (and $L_{\min}=b$).
  2. One step: $w_{t+1}=w_t-\eta\cdot2aw_t=(1-2a\eta)\,w_t$, so $w_t=(1-2a\eta)^tw_0$.
  3. Convergence needs $|1-2a\eta|<1$: $-1<1-2a\eta<1$, i.e. $0<\eta<\frac1a$.
  4. Fastest when the factor is 0: $\eta_{\text{opt}}=\frac1{2a}$ (one step).
  5. Above $\frac1{2a}$ the factor is negative (zig-zag) and its size grows again, so a larger $\eta$ can be slower.

2 · EWMA unrolled, and bias correction

  1. $S_t=\rho S_{t-1}+(1-\rho)y_t$ with $S_0=0$.
  2. Substitute $S_{t-1}$, then $S_{t-2}$, …: $S_t=(1-\rho)\big(y_t+\rho y_{t-1}+\rho^2y_{t-2}+\dots+\rho^{t-1}y_1\big)$.
  3. Sum of the weights: $(1-\rho)(1+\rho+\dots+\rho^{t-1})=1-\rho^t$ (geometric series).
  4. To get a proper weighted average the weights must sum to 1: $\hat S_t=S_t/(1-\rho^t)$.
  5. Check: $\hat S_1=\frac{(1-\rho)y_1}{1-\rho}=y_1$.

3 · Adam's first step

  1. $i=1$, $\mathbf v_0=\mathbf r_0=\mathbf 0$: $\mathbf v_1=(1-\rho_1)\mathbf g$ and $\mathbf r_1=(1-\rho_2)\mathbf g^2$.
  2. Bias correction: $\hat{\mathbf v}_1=\mathbf v_1/(1-\rho_1)=\mathbf g$ and $\hat{\mathbf r}_1=\mathbf g^2$.
  3. Step (ignoring δ): $\epsilon\,\mathbf g/\sqrt{\mathbf g^2}=\epsilon\cdot\operatorname{sign}(\mathbf g)$.
  4. Every parameter moves by exactly $\epsilon$ in the downhill direction, whatever the size of its gradient: the learning rate is a step length.

4 · Batch norm by hand, and undoing it

  1. Take one unit's values over the mini-batch, e.g. $(1,2,3,6)$.
  2. Mean $\mu=3$; variance $\sigma^2=\frac14\sum(z_i-\mu)^2=3.5$ (divide by $k$, not $k-1$).
  3. $\hat z_i=(z_i-\mu)/\sqrt{\sigma^2+\delta}$: $(-1.07,-0.53,0,1.60)$.
  4. Output $\gamma\hat z_i+\beta$ with the learned $\gamma,\beta$.
  5. With $\gamma=\sqrt{\sigma^2+\delta}$ and $\beta=\mu$: $\gamma\hat z_i+\beta=z_i$, so the network can undo the normalisation completely.

10Exam traps

Watch out
  • A larger learning rate is NOT always faster, even when it converges (24/25 exam): past $\frac1{2a}$ the zig-zag gets worse.
  • For $aw^2+b$: $\eta_{\text{opt}}=\frac1{2a}$, convergence for $0<\eta<\frac1a$. The review's label "$\eta_{\max}=\frac1{2a}$" mixes the two up.
  • Momentum = average of gradients, RMSprop = average of squared gradients, Adam = both + bias correction. Recognise Adam in pseudocode by the two EWMAs and the division by $1-\rho^i$.
  • Momentum and RMSprop are usually implemented without bias correction; Adam with.
  • Bias correction only matters at the start: $1-\rho^t\to1$.
  • RMSprop and Adam give every parameter a step of about ε: they fix differently scaled directions along the coordinate axes, not rotated ravines.
  • Two momentum conventions: with or without the factor $1-\rho$; PyTorch uses the one without.
  • EWMA memory: one extra number per parameter (slide 14: $2n$ values in total for $n$ parameters); Adam stores two.
  • Batch norm statistics are per unit, over the mini-batch, not per example over the units.
  • Batch norm at test time uses running averages from training, not the test batch's own statistics (exam review).
  • The learned γ and β can undo batch norm: TRUE (24/25 exam).
  • "Batch norm removes the need for parameter initialisation" is FALSE (24/25 exam); good initialisation still shortens training.
  • Normalise test data with the training set's μ and σ.
  • SGD noise doesn't disappear near the minimum: decay the learning rate.

11Self-check

Answer first, then open. past exam marks questions from the 24/25 exam reviews.

1. past exam $L(w)=aw^2+b$. (a) The optimal $w$? (b) The optimal learning rate? (c) How does the shape of the loss (the value of $a$) affect it?
(a) $L'=2aw=0$ gives $w_{\text{opt}}=0$. (b) $w\leftarrow(1-2a\eta)w$, so $\eta_{\text{opt}}=\frac1{2a}$ (one step); it converges for $0<\eta<\frac1a$. (c) A larger $a$ (more curvature, a narrower bowl) needs a smaller learning rate.
2. past exam True or false: if the convergence conditions are met, a larger learning rate always leads to faster convergence.
False. With $a=1$: $\eta=0.5$ converges in one step, $\eta=0.9$ converges too but shrinks $|w|$ by only a factor 0.8 per step.
3. past exam Which algorithm is this? v = p1·v + (1−p1)·g; vhat = v/(1−p1^i); r = p2·r + (1−p2)·g²; rhat = r/(1−p2^i); params −= lr·vhat/sqrt(rhat)
Adam. Momentum has no $\mathbf r$ (no adaptive step size); RMSprop has no $\mathbf v$ and no bias correction; plain SGD would be params -= lr * g.
4. past exam At test time you get a single sample, so there is no batch mean or standard deviation. How do you use batch norm?
Use the running averages of the mean and variance collected during training (an EWMA), together with the learned $\gamma$ and $\beta$.
5. past exam True or false: by allowing learnable parameters in batch normalisation, the network can undo the normalisation.
True: $\gamma=\sqrt{\sigma^2+\delta}$, $\beta=\mu$.
6. past exam Which statement about batch norm is false? (a) It normalises each unit over the mini-batch. (b) It has learnable scale and shift. (c) It removes the need for parameter initialisation. (d) It uses running statistics at test time.
(c) is false. Related past exam statement (true): proper weight initialisation can significantly reduce the time a network needs to converge.
7. EWMA with ρ = 0.9 on $y=(4,6)$: give $S_1,S_2$ and the bias-corrected values.
$S_1=0.4$, $S_2=0.9\cdot0.4+0.1\cdot6=0.96$; $\hat S_1=0.4/0.1=4$, $\hat S_2=0.96/(1-0.81)=5.05$.
8. RMSprop with ρ = 0.9 and ε = 0.1 sees a first gradient $g=3$ (δ ignored). How big is the step? And with bias correction?
$r_1=0.1\cdot9=0.9$, step $=0.1\cdot3/\sqrt{0.9}=0.316$. With bias correction $\hat r_1=0.9/0.1=9$ and the step is $0.1\cdot3/3=0.1=\epsilon$.
9. Momentum (slide version) with ρ = 0.9 and a constant gradient $g=2$: give $v_1,v_2,v_3$. Where does $v$ go?
$v_1=0.2$, $v_2=0.9\cdot0.2+0.2=0.38$, $v_3=0.542$; $v\to2=g$ (the average of a constant). The bias-corrected $\hat v_t$ equals 2 from the start.
10. A hidden layer has 128 units with batch norm. How many learnable parameters does batch norm add, and how many running statistics does it store?
$2\cdot128=256$ learnable ($\gamma,\beta$) and 256 running statistics (mean and variance per unit), which are not learned by gradient descent.

Effect of the learning rate. For gradient descent the y-direction (curvature 0.2) is stable only for ε < 10, and the x-direction (curvature 0.02) moves by a factor $1-0.02\epsilon$ per step, so with ε = 0.1 it crawls. Too small is slow, too large overshoots and diverges. For RMSprop and Adam, ε is roughly the step length per coordinate, so it should match the distance to travel and the precision you need near the end.

Effect of ρ. A larger ρ means a longer memory of about $1/(1-\rho)$ steps: smoother, but slower to react. Momentum with a large ρ keeps going and overshoots around the minimum. RMSprop with ρ = 0.999 and no bias correction starts with a tiny $\mathbf r$, so its first steps are huge: here y jumps from −2 to about +1.07 in one step.
