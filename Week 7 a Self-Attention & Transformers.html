Self-Attention and the Transformer

Self-attention is the idea behind every large language model. Its core is short: every word looks at every other word, decides how relevant each one is (a dot product and a softmax), and becomes a weighted mix of them. There is no recurrence, so all words are processed in parallel, and any two words are one step apart. Learned queries, keys and values decide who listens to whom and what is passed on; several heads capture several relations at once; position encodings put word order back in. Stack that with a small MLP, residual connections and layer norm, and you have a Transformer.

0Test yourself first

Answer out loud before you read on. Each answer points to the part that explains it.

1. You shuffle the words of a sentence and feed it to a self-attention layer that has no position information. What happens to the outputs?
You get exactly the same output vectors, shuffled in the same way (permutation equivariance). Average them into one sentence vector and the result doesn't change at all: "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" look identical. That is why position information must be added. → Part 8
2. One self-attention layer processes a 10-word sentence and then a 1000-word sentence. How many parameters does it need for each? How many attention weights does it compute?
The same parameters ($\mathbf W_q,\mathbf W_k,\mathbf W_v$ are shared by all words: slide 27). But the number of attention weights is $n^2$: $100$ versus $1\,000\,000$. Self-attention is cheap in parameters and expensive in sequence length. → Part 6
3. Why does multi-head attention make a transformer better? Is it cheaper than one head?
Each head has its own $\mathbf W_q,\mathbf W_k,\mathbf W_v$, so different heads can capture different kinds of relations at the same time ("koala eats shoots and leaves" read two ways). It is not cheaper: splitting one vector into narrow heads costs about the same as one head. (2025 MC 13, one you missed.) → Part 7

One sentence to remember

Self-attention turns a sequence of vectors into a new sequence of the same length: each output $\mathbf y_i=\sum_j\operatorname{softmax}_j\!\big(\mathbf q_i^\top\mathbf k_j/\sqrt{d_k}\big)\,\mathbf v_j$ is a weighted average of all values, with weights from how well word $i$'s query matches word $j$'s key. It is parallel, links any two words in one step, has parameters that don't depend on the sentence length, costs $n^2$ in the length, and ignores word order unless positions are added.

2The problem: long-range relations slides 3–10 D2L 11.6

Q: What is the difficulty here? (slide 3)

Learn a model that predicts the next word, for sentences like

The student, after reading the [old, thick, red, …] book, was prepared.
The students, after reading the [old, thick, red, …] book, were prepared.

A: a variable-sized, long-distance relation between "student[s]" and "was[were]". Any number of words can sit in between, and the network must still carry "one or several" across them.

Recap: why not an RNN? (slide 8)

An RNN reads one word per step with shared weights and carries a hidden state (Week 6b).

  • Q: Why are recurrent nets (RNN, GRU, LSTM) difficult to train in parallel? A: they are inherently sequential: each step depends on the previous one. You can't compute step 50 before step 49.
  • And the information (and the gradient) has to survive every step in between, which is where vanishing gradients come from.

The self-attention idea (slides 9–10)
  • Keep the word-embedding vectors at the input and the shared output weights per word.
  • Remove the recurrence, but still introduce word relationships: somehow link all words to each other, without hard-coded positional weights.
  • Q: How many words influence a single word vector in the self-attention layer? A: all of them. Each output is influenced and changed by all other words. (The purple lines on the slides are not individual weights; they are computed, as Part 4 shows.)

3Words as vectors slides 4–6 D2L 15.1 · Bloem · Rohrer

Q: How to represent input words? (slides 4–5)

The simplest answer is a one-hot vector: number the words of the vocabulary and put a 1 at the word's index. Q: What are the dot-product similarities between input words? A: 0, for every pair of different words. One-hot vectors say nothing about meaning: "student" is exactly as far from "book" as from "was". We want word embeddings: vectors whose similarities mean something.

Formula: word2vec (slide 6)

Word $i$ from vocabulary $\mathbf V$ is a one-hot row vector $\mathbf x_i$. Compute its word vector and use it to predict the context:

$$\mathbf h_i=\mathbf x_i\mathbf W_{dv}\qquad\text{(a } d\text{-dimensional real vector)},\qquad\mathbf y_i=\mathbf W_{vd}\,\mathbf h_i\qquad\text{(scores for the surrounding words)}$$
  • Skip-gram: from the centre word predict the $k$ surrounding words.
  • CBOW (continuous bag-of-words): from the $k$ surrounding words predict the centre word.

Training pushes words that appear in similar contexts ("coffee" and "tea") towards similar vectors. After training, $\mathbf W_{dv}$ is the embedding table: row $i$ is word $i$'s vector.

From the blog: the dot product as a match score (Bloem)

Bloem explains dot products with a movie recommender. Give every movie two numbers, how romantic it is and how much action it has, and give every user two numbers, how much they like romance and action. The dot product of user and movie adds up the matches: if both numbers are positive (a romantic movie, a user who loves romance) the term is positive; if the signs differ (a romantic movie, a user who hates romance) it is negative; large numbers count more. In practice nobody labels the features: they are learned from which users liked which movies, and they turn out to mean something. Word embeddings work the same way, and in self-attention the "user" is the word asking, the "movies" are the words being looked at.

From 3Blue1Brown: meaning lives in directions, but not yet in context

In a trained model, directions in embedding space carry meaning (one direction for "plural", one for "feminine", and so on). But the first lookup gives a word the same vector in every sentence: "mole" gets one vector whether you mean the animal, the unit of chemistry or the spot on your skin. Attention is the step where the surrounding words push each vector towards the meaning that fits the context.

4Self-attention, step by step slides 11–17 Bloem · D2L 11.6

Definition (slide 11)

Self-attention is a sequence-to-sequence operation: input vectors $\mathbf x_1,\dots,\mathbf x_n$ of size $d$ go in, output vectors $\mathbf y_1,\dots,\mathbf y_n$ of size $d$ come out. Start with words (the, student, reads, a, book), look up their learned embeddings ($\mathbf x_{\text{the}},\dots$), and self-attention converts them to $\mathbf y_{\text{the}},\dots,\mathbf y_{\text{book}}$: learning how to re-weigh words with all other words.

Q: Why is self-attention parallelizable? A: no recurrence; each output vector can be computed independently, so all of them in parallel.

1 · Similarities (slides 12–15)For every pair: $w'_{ij}=\mathbf x_i^\top\mathbf x_j$. With $n$ words that is $n^2$ numbers, unbounded in $(-\infty,\infty)$.
2 · Softmax (slides 15–16)$w_{ij}=\dfrac{\exp w'_{ij}}{\sum_j\exp w'_{ij}}$: non-negative, and $\sum_jw_{ij}=1$ for each output word $i$.
3 · Weighted sum (slides 16–17)$\mathbf y_i=\sum_jw_{ij}\,\mathbf x_j$: the new vector for word $i$ is a mix of all word vectors.

Rows or columns? (slide 16)

On slide 16 the rows of the weight matrix don't sum to 1, because the slide draws one column per output word, so its softmax runs down the columns. In the formulas and in code, row $i$ belongs to output word $i$: $w_{ij}$ is normalised over $j$, which is softmax(..., dim=2) for a tensor of shape (b, t, t). Same numbers, transposed picture. 3Blue1Brown also draws it column-wise and warns about the same thing.

From the blog: basic self-attention has no parameters (Bloem)
  • The weights $w_{ij}$ are not parameters: they are computed from the inputs, fresh for every sentence. Basic self-attention learns nothing by itself; everything depends on the embeddings that come in.
  • For "the cat walks on the street" you would expect "the" to have small or negative dot products with most words, and "cat" and "walks" (a subject and its verb) a large one. Learning the embeddings is what makes that true.
  • It is the only operation in a transformer that moves information between positions; everything else acts on each vector separately.

5Queries, keys and values slides 19–27 Bloem · 3Blue1Brown · D2L 11.1

Two roles of a word vector (slides 19–20)

A word vector is used in two roles: (1) computing a weight for how much a word A influences a word B, and (2) being summed into a new vector.

  • Example of role 1 (which words should influence each other?): (the, student), (the, book), (student, was); not so much (the, was). The "types" that influence each other: (noun, verb), (article, noun), …
  • Example of role 2 (what should "student[s]" give to allow "was[were]"?): information about the concept "singular/plural".

Q: Is the required information the same for both roles? A: no. Role 1 needs the type of word (noun, verb); role 2 needs what to give (plurality). One vector shouldn't have to do both, so give each its own projection.

Formula: self-attention with learned roles (slide 26)

Pre-compute, for each word $\mathbf x_i$, with three learnable matrices (the same for every word):

$$\mathbf q_i=\mathbf W_q\mathbf x_i,\qquad\mathbf k_i=\mathbf W_k\mathbf x_i,\qquad\mathbf v_i=\mathbf W_v\mathbf x_i$$

Fill the $n^2$ matrix of all word pairs, normalise, and mix the values:

$$w'_{ij}=\mathbf q_i^\top\mathbf k_j,\qquad w_{ij}=\frac{\exp w'_{ij}}{\sum_j\exp w'_{ij}},\qquad\mathbf y_i=\sum_jw_{ij}\,\mathbf v_j$$
  • Where do $\mathbf W_q,\mathbf W_k$ go? Into the similarity, step 1 (slide 23): query of the output word, key of the input word.
  • Where does $\mathbf W_v$ go? Into the weighted sum, step 3 (slide 25): what gets mixed are values, not raw word vectors.
  • In practice also divide $w'_{ij}$ by $\sqrt{d_k}$ (Part 6). Assignment 11 does.

From 3Blue1Brown: the fluffy blue creature

In "a fluffy blue creature roamed the verdant forest", imagine one head in which every noun's query asks "are there adjectives in front of me?", and adjectives' keys answer "yes, I'm an adjective". The query of "creature" then matches the keys of "fluffy" and "blue", and their values, added to the embedding of "creature", move it towards "a fluffy blue creature". Each head proposes such a change $\Delta\mathbf E$ for every position. Real heads are learned from data and are rarely this easy to read, but the mechanism is exactly this.

Q: If the sentence gets longer, how do the parameters change? (slide 27)

A: no change. All learnable parameters ($\mathbf W_q,\mathbf W_k,\mathbf W_v$, and $\mathbf W_{hq}$ at the output) are shared over the words. A sentence of 5 or 5,000 words uses the same $3d^2$ attention parameters. What grows is the $n\times n$ weight matrix (Part 6).

6Scaling, matrices, cost, masks Bloem · Assignment 11 · 3Blue1Brown

Why divide by the square root of the dimension?

Formula: scaled dot-product attention
$$w'_{ij}=\frac{\mathbf q_i^\top\mathbf k_j}{\sqrt{d_k}}$$

A dot product adds up $d_k$ products. If the entries are roughly independent with variance 1, the sum has variance $d_k$, so typical scores are $\pm\sqrt{d_k}$ in size: about $\pm22$ for $d_k=512$. A softmax of such large numbers is almost one-hot, and its derivative is then almost zero: learning stalls ("it kills the gradient", Assignment 11). Dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ brings the scores back to size about 1 for any dimension. Bloem's version of the argument: a vector with all entries $c$ has length $\sqrt{d}\,c$, so dividing by $\sqrt d$ removes the growth.

All words at once: the matrix form

Formula: self-attention in matrix form (rows = words)
$$\mathbf Q=\mathbf X\mathbf W_q,\quad\mathbf K=\mathbf X\mathbf W_k,\quad\mathbf V=\mathbf X\mathbf W_v,\qquad\mathbf Y=\operatorname{softmax}\!\Big(\frac{\mathbf Q\mathbf K^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\Big)\mathbf V\quad\text{(softmax over each row)}$$

With a batch the tensors get a leading dimension: x is (b, t, k), $\mathbf Q\mathbf K^\top$ is torch.bmm(q, k.transpose(1, 2)) of shape (b, t, t), and the output torch.bmm(w, v) is (b, t, k) again. Assignment 11 A7.1–A7.2 is exactly this (walkthrough).

What it costs

Tiny numbers: parameters stay, the n by n matrix grows
  • Parameters (no biases): $\mathbf W_q,\mathbf W_k,\mathbf W_v$ have $3d^2$ numbers: $3\cdot512^2=786\,432$ for $d=512$, for any sentence length.
  • Attention weights: $n^2$ per head and layer: $10^6$ for $n=1000$. Bloem's example: a batch of 16 such matrices in 32-bit floats is $16\cdot10^6\cdot4$ bytes $=64$ MB, and training stores several per head per layer. This is why context length is the bottleneck of large language models (3Blue1Brown).
  • GPT-3 (3Blue1Brown): embedding size 12,288, queries and keys of size 128, 96 heads per layer, 96 layers. Its attention weights add up to almost 58 billion parameters, about a third of the 175 billion total; most of the rest sit in the MLPs.

Masking: don't look at the future

Causal masking (slide 30: "masks the future")

A language model is trained to predict the next word at every position at once: one sentence of 100 words gives 100 training examples in a single pass (3Blue1Brown). That only works if position $i$ can't see words $i+1,i+2,\dots$, otherwise it could simply copy the answer. So before the softmax set $w'_{ij}=-\infty$ for all $j>i$; after the softmax those weights are exactly 0 and each row still sums to 1. In code (Bloem): dot[:, i, j] = float('-inf') for the upper triangle, e.g. with torch.triu_indices(t, t, offset=1). Switch on "mask the future" in the widget of Part 4 to see it.

7Multi-head attention slide 28 Bloem · D2L 11.5 · 3Blue1Brown

Q: What do you notice about this sentence? (slide 28)

The, koala, eats, shoots, and, leaves.

A: two readings: (eating shoots, and eating leaves) — a koala's dinner; or (it eats, then it shoots, then it leaves) — a koala with a gun. Q: How is this currently modelled? A: it is not. With one set of $\mathbf W_q,\mathbf W_k,\mathbf W_v$ every word gets one attention pattern, so "eats" can't link to its objects and to its subject in two separate ways: both get mixed into one vector. Bloem's example is the same: in "Mary gave roses to Susan", "gave" relates to Mary (the giver), roses (the thing) and Susan (the receiver) in three different ways.

Formula: multiple heads

Use $h$ heads: multiple variants $\mathbf W_q^r,\mathbf W_k^r,\mathbf W_v^r$, indexed by $r$. Each head computes its own attention and output $\mathbf y_i^r$; concatenate and mix with one more matrix:

$$\mathbf y_i=\mathbf W_o\big[\mathbf y_i^1;\,\mathbf y_i^2;\,\dots;\,\mathbf y_i^h\big]$$
  • Narrow: divide the vector into $h$ chunks, e.g. turn one 256-d vector into eight 32-d heads. Same cost as a single head.
  • Wide: apply each head independently at full size, concatenate and project back, e.g. run a 256-d attention 8 times and project 2048-d back to 256-d. About $h$ times the cost; this is Assignment 11's MultiHeadAttention.

Exam answer (2025 MC 13 and 24/25 #35)

Multi-head attention improves a transformer because it lets the model capture different kinds of relationships between tokens simultaneously, each head focusing on a different aspect of the input. It does not reduce the cost: splitting one vector into narrow heads costs the same as one head (and wide heads cost more). GPT-3 runs 96 heads in every layer.

8Word order: positions slide 29 Bloem · D2L 11.6 · Rohrer

Q: If I change the word order, do I get different results? (slide 29)

A: no. Dot products and sums don't care about position. Strictly: self-attention is permutation equivariant (shuffle the inputs and the outputs are the same vectors in the shuffled order, Assignment 11's word), and anything that pools over the positions, like an average, is permutation invariant (the slide's word). Either way, "dog bites man" and "man bites dog" can't be told apart. Q: A solution? Add position information to the input vectors.

Two options (slide 29)
  • Learned position embedding: embed the positions like words: just as you learn $\mathbf x_{\text{cat}}$ and $\mathbf x_{\text{Susan}}$, learn $\mathbf x_{12}$ and $\mathbf x_{25}$ and add them to the word vectors. Simple and effective (Assignment 11 uses nn.Embedding(input_length, k)), but a position never seen in training has an untrained vector, so longer sentences than in training are a problem (Bloem).
  • Position encoding: construct a fixed function $f:\mathbb N\to\mathbb R^d$ and let the network learn to use it. The original transformer uses sines and cosines at different frequencies (D2L 11.6):
$$p_{i,2j}=\sin\!\Big(\frac{i}{10000^{2j/d}}\Big),\qquad p_{i,2j+1}=\cos\!\Big(\frac{i}{10000^{2j/d}}\Big)\qquad(i=\text{position},\ 2j,2j+1=\text{dimension})$$

Each pair of dimensions $(2j,2j+1)$ is a clock hand at angle $i/10000^{2j/d}$: the first pair turns one radian per position, later pairs ever slower, like the second, minute and hour hands of a clock, or the fast and slow bits of a binary counter (D2L). Together the hands give every position a unique pattern (Rohrer's "circular wiggle"). Bottom right: the dot product of position $i$'s encoding with every other position's, scaled so that position $i$ itself scores 1. Try: slide the position: the curve always peaks at $i$ and falls off with distance in the same way, because for these encodings the dot product depends only on the distance between two positions.

Tiny numbers: positions 1 and 2 with four dimensions

$d=4$: the frequencies are $1/10000^{0}=1$ and $1/10000^{2/4}=0.01$.

$\mathbf p_1=(\sin1,\cos1,\sin0.01,\cos0.01)=(0.84,\;0.54,\;0.01,\;1.00)$, $\quad\mathbf p_2=(\sin2,\cos2,\sin0.02,\cos0.02)=(0.91,\;-0.42,\;0.02,\;1.00)$.

These are added to the word vectors, $\mathbf x_i+\mathbf p_i$, not concatenated, so the shapes don't change.

9The Transformer slide 30 Vaswani et al. 2017 · Bloem · Rohrer

Part (slide 30)What it does
Word / token embeddingsLook up a learned vector per token (Part 3).
Positional encodingAdd location information (Part 8).
Multi-head (self-)attentionTokens exchange information (Parts 4–7). In the decoder it masks the future.
Add & normA residual connection plus a layer norm, "not batch norm but another" (figure below).
Feed forwardThe same MLP for each token: linear → ReLU → linear, hidden size $4d$.
Encoder / decoderThe encoder reads the input without a mask; the decoder generates, with cross-attention into the encoder.
Linear"De-embed" to the vocabulary size: one score per word.
SoftmaxNormalise to word probabilities.
Outputs (shifted right)Next-token prediction: the target sequence is the input sequence shifted by one.
Why each piece is there
  • Self-attention is the only place where tokens exchange information; the MLP then processes what each token has collected, on its own. Rohrer's view: attention selects which earlier words matter, and the MLP turns the selected combination into features and predictions.
  • Residual connections keep the original input available (attention may ignore the token's own word) and keep gradients flowing through deep stacks (Week 6a).
  • Layer norm keeps every token's vector in a stable range for the next softmax and ReLU, independent of the batch.
  • Bloem's guiding principle: almost everything is linear except the softmax and the ReLU, which keeps gradients healthy, so the design can go deep.
Encoder-only, decoder-only, and a classifier (Bloem, Rohrer)
  • Encoder-decoder (the original, for translation): the encoder reads the source sentence; every decoder layer uses cross-attention, with queries from the decoder and keys and values from the encoder.
  • Encoder-only (BERT): a stack of blocks without a mask, trained to predict masked-out words from both sides. Largest version: 24 blocks, size 1024, 16 heads, 340M parameters.
  • Decoder-only (GPT-2, GPT-3): a stack of masked blocks that predicts the next token. GPT-2: 48 blocks, context 1024, size 1600, 1.5B parameters.
  • Classifier (Bloem's IMDb model, Assignment 11): embeddings + position embeddings → blocks → average over the positions → linear layer to the class scores. Bloem reaches about 85% on IMDb sentiment with 6 blocks.
  • Tokens are often word pieces: byte-pair encoding merges frequent character pairs, so "transformer" may be one token and a rare word several (Rohrer).

10Recap

What to know for the exam (slide 32)
  • Word embeddings: one-hot vectors have no similarity (all dot products 0); word2vec learns vectors from context (skip-gram, CBOW).
  • Self-attention: $w'_{ij}=\mathbf x_i^\top\mathbf x_j$ for all $n^2$ pairs, softmax over $j$, $\mathbf y_i=\sum_jw_{ij}\mathbf x_j$. No recurrence: parallel, any two words one step apart.
  • Queries, keys, values: $\mathbf q_i=\mathbf W_q\mathbf x_i$, $\mathbf k_i=\mathbf W_k\mathbf x_i$, $\mathbf v_i=\mathbf W_v\mathbf x_i$; $w'_{ij}=\mathbf q_i^\top\mathbf k_j$ (in practice $/\sqrt{d_k}$), $\mathbf y_i=\sum_jw_{ij}\mathbf v_j$. Parameters don't depend on the length.
  • Multi-head: several $\mathbf W_q^r,\mathbf W_k^r,\mathbf W_v^r$ capture different relations simultaneously; narrow (split) or wide (full size, project back). Not cheaper.
  • Position encodings: self-attention ignores order; add learned position embeddings or a fixed function such as sinusoids.
  • Transformer: embeddings + positions → blocks of (masked) multi-head attention and a per-token MLP, each with residual + layer norm → linear → softmax.

3 · Shapes in Assignment 11's wide multi-head attention

  1. Input x: (b, t, k), e.g. (4, 5, 6) with $h=3$ heads.
  2. toqueries(x): (b, t, h·k) → .view(b, t, h, k) → .transpose(1, 2): (b, h, t, k) → .reshape(b·h, t, k): (12, 5, 6).
  3. Scores bmm(q, k.transpose(1, 2)): (b·h, t, t) = (12, 5, 5); softmax over dim=2; bmm(w, v): (12, 5, 6).
  4. Unfold: .view(b, h, t, k) → .transpose(1, 2) → .reshape(b, t, h·k) = (4, 5, 18) → unifyheads: (4, 5, 6).

4 · Counting the parameters of a transformer block ($k=64$, 4 wide heads)

  1. Queries, keys, values: $3\times(64\times256)=49{,}152$ (no bias).
  2. unifyheads: $256\times64+64=16{,}448$. Attention total: $65{,}600$.
  3. MLP: $64\times256+256+256\times64+64=33{,}088$. Two layer norms: $2\times(64+64)=256$.
  4. Block: $65{,}600+33{,}088+256=98{,}944$, independent of the sentence length (checked with PyTorch).

5 · A sinusoidal position encoding by hand

  1. $d=4$, position $i=1$; pairs $j=0$ and $j=1$.
  2. Frequencies: $1/10000^{0}=1$ and $1/10000^{2/4}=1/100$.
  3. $\mathbf p_1=(\sin1,\;\cos1,\;\sin0.01,\;\cos0.01)=(0.84,\;0.54,\;0.01,\;1.00)$.
  4. Add it to the word vector at position 1: $\mathbf x_1+\mathbf p_1$.

6 · A causal mask on three words

  1. Scores $w'$ (rows = output word): $\begin{bmatrix}1&2&0\\0&1&3\\2&1&1\end{bmatrix}$.
  2. Mask: set every entry above the diagonal to $-\infty$: row 1 keeps only $w'_{11}$, row 2 keeps $w'_{21},w'_{22}$.
  3. Softmax per row: row 1 $=(1,0,0)$; row 2 $=(e^0,e^1)/(e^0+e^1)=(0.27,\;0.73,\;0)$; row 3 unchanged: $(0.58,\;0.21,\;0.21)$.
  4. Every row still sums to 1, and no word took anything from a later word.

12Exam traps

Watch out
  • One-hot vectors have dot product 0 for every pair of different words: no notion of similarity.
  • Basic self-attention has no parameters. The weights $w_{ij}$ are computed from the input, not learned.
  • $n$ words give $n^2$ similarities, but the number of parameters doesn't depend on $n$ (slide 27).
  • The softmax normalises over $j$ (the words being looked at), for each output word $i$: rows in code, columns in the slides' picture.
  • The output is a weighted sum of values, not of queries or keys. $\mathbf W_q,\mathbf W_k$ go into the similarity, $\mathbf W_v$ into the sum.
  • Scale by $\sqrt{d_k}$, not $d_k$, to keep the softmax from saturating. (Bloem's current code scales by the per-head size.)
  • Self-attention is parallel, RNNs are sequential (slides 8, 11). Self-attention's path between any two words has length 1.
  • Multi-head attention is about capability, not cost (2025 MC 13): narrow heads cost the same as one head; wide heads cost $h$ times more.
  • Without positions, word order is invisible. Self-attention is permutation equivariant; after pooling, invariant. The slide uses the second word for both.
  • Position vectors are added to the word vectors, not concatenated.
  • The decoder's self-attention is masked (no peeking at later tokens); the encoder's is not. Cross-attention: queries from the decoder, keys and values from the encoder.
  • Transformers use layer norm, not batch norm (slide 30), and the MLP is applied to every token separately with the same weights.
  • Self-attention costs $O(n^2d)$ time and $n^2$ memory per head: that, not the parameter count, limits the context length.

13Self-check

Answer first, then open. past exam marks questions from the exam reviews.

1. past exam True or false: multi-head self-attention in transformer models is for capturing diverse relationships by focusing on different aspects of the input simultaneously. (24/25 #35)
True. Every head has its own $\mathbf W_q^r,\mathbf W_k^r,\mathbf W_v^r$ and so its own attention pattern.
2. past exam Which statement best describes the primary advantage of self-attention? (24/25 #52)
It lets the model weigh the importance of different parts of the input dynamically, capturing long-range dependencies regardless of their distance in the sequence. The weights are computed per input, and any two positions are one step apart.
3. past exam Why does multi-head attention improve the capability of a transformer? (2025 MC 13, missed)
It enables the model to capture different kinds of relationships or features between tokens simultaneously. Not: "it reduces the cost by splitting the work over heads". Splitting gives the same cost, and lower cost wouldn't be more capability anyway.
4. past exam In the 2025 coding question, Task A asks for scaled dot-product self-attention with $\mathbf W'=\mathbf Q\mathbf K^\top/\sqrt k$ and $\mathbf W=\operatorname{softmax}(\mathbf W',\text{dim}=2)$. Why dim=2?
The scores have shape (B, T, T): batch, output word $i$, input word $j$. The weights of one output word must sum to 1 over the words it looks at, the last dimension. The full question is worked through in Week 7b, exercise E2.
5. Why are RNNs difficult to train in parallel, and why is self-attention not?
Each RNN step needs the previous hidden state, so the steps must run one after another. A self-attention output depends only on the input vectors, so all outputs can be computed at once with matrix products (slides 8 and 11).
6. A sentence has 50 words. How many similarities does one self-attention layer compute per head?
$50^2=2500$ (all words to all words, slide 13).
7. What do the query, key and value of a word stand for? Give the slides' example.
Query: what the word looks for (its "type" when it influences, e.g. noun-ness); key: what it can be matched on by others (e.g. verb-ness); value: what it gives when chosen (e.g. plurality). The dot product of a query and a key decides the weight; the values are what gets summed.
8. In a decoder, why is the self-attention masked, and how is the mask applied?
During training the model predicts the next token at every position at once; without a mask, position $i$ could look at token $i+1$ and copy the answer. Set the scores $w'_{ij}=-\infty$ for $j>i$ before the softmax, so those weights become 0.
9. Give one advantage of learned position embeddings and one of a fixed position encoding.
Learned embeddings are simple and adapt to the data. A fixed function such as sinusoids is defined for every position, so it can extend to sequences longer than those seen in training; with sinusoids the similarity of two positions depends only on their distance.
10. Narrow multi-head attention with $d=512$ and $h=8$: what size are each head's queries, and how many parameters do $\mathbf W_q,\mathbf W_k,\mathbf W_v$ have together?
Each head works in $512/8=64$ dimensions. Together still $3\cdot512^2=786{,}432$: the same as a single head, just cut into 8 pieces.
11. What is the difference between layer norm and batch norm, and why do transformers use layer norm?
Batch norm normalises each feature over the batch; layer norm normalises each token's vector over its own features. Layer norm doesn't depend on the batch or on padded sentence lengths and acts the same at training and test time.
12. Why divide the dot products by $\sqrt{d_k}$?
The dot product of two random $d_k$-dimensional vectors has a spread of about $\sqrt{d_k}$; big scores make the softmax nearly one-hot, and then its gradient is nearly zero. Dividing by $\sqrt{d_k}$ keeps the scores around size 1.

A7.1 · Basic self-attention

Implement forward for input x of shape [b, t, k]: $w'_{ij}=\mathbf x_i^\top\mathbf x_j$, $w_{ij}=\operatorname{softmax}_j(w'_{ij})$, $\mathbf y_i=\sum_jw_{ij}\mathbf x_j$. No parameters. Hint in the notebook: use torch.bmm.

Hint

All $w'_{ij}$ at once is $\mathbf X\mathbf X^\top$ per batch element: bmm of x with x transposed in its last two dimensions gives (b, t, t). Which dimension must the softmax sum over? The weighted sum is one more bmm.

Show worked solution
w_prime = torch.bmm(x, x.transpose(1, 2))     # (b, t, t): all dot products

y = torch.bmm(w, x)                            # (b, t, k): weighted sums of the inputs

A7.3 · Wide multi-head attention

Now tokeys, toqueries, tovalues map k to k * heads, and unifyheads maps k * heads back to k. Reshape to (b, t, h, k), fold the heads into the batch to get (b·h, t, k), do the attention with bmm, unfold, concatenate the heads and unify.

Hint

Blueprint 3 has every shape. The key move is .transpose(1, 2) to put the heads next to the batch before .reshape(b * h, t, k); a plain reshape without the transpose would mix the time steps of different heads. Use .reshape (or .contiguous().view) after a transpose.

Show worked solution
queries = self.toqueries(x).view(b, t, h, k)       # every head gets its own k-dim q, k, v
keys    = self.tokeys(x).view(b, t, h, k)
values  = self.tovalues(x).view(b, t, h, k)
queries = queries.transpose(1, 2).reshape(b * h, t, k)   # fold the heads into the batch
keys    = keys.transpose(1, 2).reshape(b * h, t, k)
values  = values.transpose(1, 2).reshape(b * h, t, k)
w_prime = torch.bmm(queries, keys.transpose(1, 2)) / math.sqrt(k)   # (b*h, t, t)
w = F.softmax(w_prime, dim=2)
out = torch.bmm(w, values).view(b, h, t, k)              # unfold
out = out.transpose(1, 2).reshape(b, t, h * k)           # concatenate the heads per word
y = self.unifyheads(out)                                 # (b, t, k)

Each head has full size $k$, so the scaling is $\sqrt k$. In Bloem's current narrow version each head has size $s=k/h$ and the scaling is $\sqrt s$. Check: "Output is correct: True".

A7.5 · Position embeddings and the Transformer classifier

The Transformer class is given. Read its forward: token embedding, plus a learned position embedding of torch.arange(t), then the blocks, then x.mean(dim=1), then self.output_projector. Question: what does self.output_projector do and why is it necessary?

Hint

Follow the shapes: what is the shape after the mean over dim=1, and what does nn.CrossEntropyLoss need?

Show worked solution

After the blocks the tensor is (b, t, k); averaging over the positions gives one $k$-dimensional vector per review, (b, k). The transformer only ever produces vectors of the embedding size $k$, but the task needs one score per class (num_outputs = 2 for positive and negative). output_projector = nn.Linear(k, num_outputs) maps the pooled vector to these class scores (logits), which nn.CrossEntropyLoss turns into probabilities and a loss.

Two more things to notice: without the position embedding, the mean over positions would make the classifier blind to word order (Part 8); and with the settings of A7.6 ($k=64$, 4 heads, 4 layers, vocabulary 50,000, length 512) the model has 3,628,674 parameters, of which 3,200,000 (88%) are the word-embedding table. Each block has only 98,944.
